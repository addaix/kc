# Research detail: conflict, staleness and time

Supporting detail for [../research.md](../research.md). Claims are graded strong / moderate / weak / contested.

## Conflict, staleness and time

**Marker convention.** Findings drawn from the bi-temporal / belief-revision topic review are marked **†**. That review did **not** receive an adversarial verification pass; its venue attributions, quotations and numeric claims have not been independently re-checked, so every † claim below is stated one grade more conservatively than its source review graded it. The knowledge-conflict review *was* adversarially verified, and where the two reviews disagree — they do, twice, on ClashEval's headline number and on truth-discovery parameter sensitivity — the verified reading wins and the disagreement is recorded rather than smoothed.

### The taxonomy: which conflicts are the store's problem

Conflict has two orthogonal axes: **locus** (where the disagreement lives) and **cause** (why it exists). Locus is canonical; cause is a design convenience with synthetic backing.

| Axis | Values | Evidence | What kc can represent |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Locus** [1][2] | context-memory (retrieved vs parametric), inter-context (two sources disagree), intra-memory (model self-inconsistency) | **Strong** as vocabulary, **not** as evidence — both surveys are preprints; authority comes from near-universal adoption, not peer review | Only **inter-context** is representable in a knowledge layer. Context-memory is a consumer-side property you can only influence; intra-memory is inside the weights and cannot be represented at all. This bounds the schema. |
| **Cause** [3] | misinformation, temporal discrepancy, semantic divergence (different sense/scope/jurisdiction) | **Moderate** — ConflictBank is 7.45M synthetic claim-evidence pairs over 12 LLMs; the partition is asserted by construction, not induced from real corpora, and the paper still carries "Under Review" ~2 years post-posting | The correct response differs per cause: *expire*, *down-weight*, *disambiguate*. A single `conflict: bool` is under-specified; a `(locus, cause)` pair is the minimum useful key. |

The practical warning inside the cause axis: telling genuine contradiction from **scope difference** at index time is unestablished. Gokul et al. find contradiction-detection performance "varying significantly across different types of contradictions" — and they had to *generate* simulated contradictions because naturally-occurring ones were not available at scale [4].

### What models actually do with conflicting evidence

The popular one-liner ("LLMs over-trust retrieved context") is **contested**, and the honest statement is narrower. The 2021 encoder-QA picture it replaced — "over-reliance on memorized information" [5] — genuinely no longer describes instruction-tuned LLMs uniformly, but the reversal is not clean.

| Claim | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Models are receptive to coherent counter-evidence — *and* show "a strong confirmation bias when the external evidence contains some information that is consistent with their parametric memory" | Xie et al., ICLR 2024 spotlight — **both halves are in the same abstract**; the widely-circulated version quotes only the first | **Contested** (two-sided by construction) [6] |
| Which knowledge wins depends on **entity popularity**: unaugmented models remain competitive on head entities; retrieval rescues the tail | Mallen et al., ACL 2023 | Moderate–strong [7] |
| "LLMs override their own correct prior >60% of the time" | ClashEval, unreplicated preprint. The adversarial pass flags this as a **response rate to deliberately planted errors**, not a base rate — the construction creates the quantity it measures | **Weak as a number**, directionally useful [8] |
| Contradiction among sources moves model confidence "only marginally" | Chen, Zhang & Choi, EMNLP 2022 | Moderate [9] |
| Models notice *that* a conflict exists but cannot localise it or answer with distinct viewpoints | Wang et al., COLM 2024 (three desiderata: identify / pinpoint / answer distinctly); WikiContradict (253 human-annotated real Wikipedia instances, 3,500+ human judgements) — worst on **implicit** conflicts requiring reasoning; WhoQA, Findings of EMNLP 2024 (5,000 questions from same-name entity collisions) | **Strong** — three independent groups, two peer-reviewed [10][11][12] |
| Models prefer LLM-*generated* context over retrieved context even when the generated context is wrong, because generated text is more question-similar and retrieval's chunking "disrupts completeness" | Tan et al., ACL 2024 Main; single unreplicated study | Moderate [13] |
| Models "often favor conflicting evidence without justification" when two retrieved sources clash | ECon, EMNLP 2024 | Moderate †[14] |
| Ordinary RAG can fall **below** no-retrieval in the worst case | Astute RAG, ACL 2025 main — its method is "the only RAG method that achieves performance comparable to or even surpassing conventional use of LLMs under the worst-case scenario" | Moderate (one strong venue, one qualitative claim) [15] |

Three design consequences survive the downgrade. **(1)** Correctness of retrieved content is a property the store must own, because the consumer demonstrably does not filter it reliably. **(2)** Handing two contradictory chunks to a prompt and asking for a balanced answer does not work — if disagreement is to be surfaced, the *spans* and the *per-claim source set* must be computed upstream. **(3)** Any agent loop that writes its own notes back into the store creates a privileged, unverified, fluent source that will out-compete fragmented primary evidence; model-generated content needs a distinct provenance class and a down-weight [13].

Do not plan to detect conflict from generator confidence alone — but do not discard confidence either. The strongest published result on exactly this question **fuses** an evidence-side conflict signal with generator confidence and gains 7.2–33.4 points of selective accuracy in incorrect-only conditions [16] (preprint). Conflict detection over the candidate set (e.g. NLI over top-k) is the load-bearing half; confidence is a usable component, not a detector. A white-box alternative exists but is background for most designs: conflict signals are linearly detectable in the residual stream before generation, with "significantly different patterns when the model relies on contextual knowledge versus parametric knowledge" [17] — a NeurIPS 2024 *workshop* paper, the weakest peer-review tier, requiring open weights and activation access.

### Staleness: semantic relevance systematically prefers the stale document

This is the cleanest positive answer in the whole section to "should the layer model time explicitly?"

- Re-rankers show "a strong bias toward older, semantically rich documents, even when they are factually obsolete" — plausibly because older documents accrete more elaborated prose and therefore more semantic mass [18]. **Caveat: the finding and its proposed fix come from the same industry lab**, and the paper is a preprint.
- Accuracy drops up to **31%** on evolving-knowledge questions across 3 real timestamped corpora (AWS release notes, Azure changes, WHO outbreak reports) over 12 open and closed models [19]; GPT-3 "tends to return outdated answers when retrieved documents do not provide sufficient information" [20].
- Outdated content that merely *coexists* with current content actively degrades output: HoH finds it "substantially reduces response accuracy by distracting models from correct information" and "can mislead models into generating potentially harmful outputs, **even when current information is available**" †[21]; a controlled clinical study stratifying PubMed abstracts by publication year concludes "retrieval similarity alone is insufficient for reliable medical RAG" †[22].
- The sharpest second-order result: **the facts where recency matters most are the ones the consumer most resists updating**. DynamicQA (Findings of EMNLP 2024) shows temporally-dynamic facts carry more intra-memory conflict and are "harder to update with context, suggesting that retrieval-augmented generation will struggle with the most commonly adapted facts" [23].

Together these say: recency must be an **explicit signal** — a filter, a rank prior, or a validity interval — enforced at retrieval, not left to the generator to discount. "Retrieve and let relevance sort it out" systematically serves stale facts.

Expiry should be **per assertion**, not a document TTL. Zhang & Choi (EMNLP 2023) predict "how long a given fact will remain true" and show that "modeling fact duration improves calibration ... under temporal misalignment, by discarding volatile facts" [24]. A blanket 90-day TTL does the opposite of what is wanted: it evicts stable facts and retains volatile ones. An expiry date can also be *predicted* rather than recorded †[25][26], but the evidence base there is two small papers — treat a predicted expiry as a low-confidence hint that triggers review, never an automatic deletion trigger.

Time-stamping content is itself a tested intervention: jointly modelling text with its timestamp improves both memorisation and **calibration** on unseen future periods, and allows refresh without retraining †[27]; temporal misalignment effects across 8 tasks / 4 domains / 5+ years are "stronger ... than have been previously reported", and continued pretraining on newer data helps far less than finetuning on target-period data †[28]. Continuously-refreshed benchmarks exist to keep measuring this †[20][29]. Read as an argument for putting effort into the store rather than the model.

### Does recency or provenance weighting recover accuracy?

| Intervention | Result | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Credibility as a **computed weight on attention** (CrAM, AAAI 2025) | >20% improvement under misinformation pollution on NQ/TriviaQA across three model families, beating supervised fine-tuning | Moderate [30] |
| Credibility as a **training signal** (CAG, EMNLP 2024 Main) | Works; achieved by training on credibility-transformed data, not prompting | Moderate [31] |
| Source credibility in fact-checking (CONFACT, IJCAI 2025) | "Effectively incorporating source credibility significantly enhances the ability of RAG models to resolve conflicting evidence" | Moderate [32] |
| Credibility as a **prompt-level label** | Preference for institutionally-corroborated sources exists across 13 open-weight models but "can be reversed by simply repeating information from less credible sources" | Moderate; **this is the failure mode** [33] |
| Reordering passages so the trusted/freshest one sits at a favoured position | Positional bias "is actually marginal in real scenarios"; strategies rearranging by LLM positional preference "do not perform better than random shuffling" | Moderate [34] |
| Instructing the model to abstain or say "sources disagree" | Insufficient; large models "often output incorrect answers instead of abstaining when the context is not [sufficient]" | Moderate [35][10] |
| Learned source-reliability / truth discovery | Not solved on real data; see below | Moderate-negative [36][37][38] |

Three things fall out. **Credibility must be a numeric, machine-readable field consumed by the ranker or the decoder — never free text in the chunk body.** The mechanism that works is weighting or training; annotation is what fails. **Deduplication is a correctness control, not a storage optimisation**: an index holding 40 syndicated copies of a low-credibility claim and one authoritative rebuttal produces the low-credibility answer regardless of attached trust weights, because repetition reverses the preference [33]. Count *distinct sources*, not documents; cap items per source and per side. And **credibility signals must derive from provenance metadata (who / when / where), not from text-intrinsic features**, because stylistic plausibility rather than factual support drives adoption, and style is exactly what an attacker controls [39][40][41].

On the machinery: do not build a learned source-credibility scorer as v1. The first systematic implementation-and-comparison of 12 truth-discovery algorithms exists precisely because none had been done †[36]; on two Deep Web domains chosen *because* they were believed clean and consequential, the authors found "a large amount of inconsistency ... and also some sources with quite low accuracy", and framed the paper as "is the problem solved?" — answered no †[37]; at 1.6B triples from 12 extractors, extraction noise turns out to be a qualitatively different error source from the source-conflict noise the fusion methods were built for †[38]. The defensible v1 is **count the independent sources and expose the count**; any weighting scheme must beat that on your own data before shipping. The ~15-year truth-discovery line is surveyed in [42] (itself flagged as cited-by-others rather than read in the source review). The mature idea worth borrowing is *copy detection* — Dong, Berti-Equille & Srivastava explicitly model sources copying from one another [43], which is the formal version of the syndication problem above.

### Corpus poisoning, honestly scoped

PoisonedRAG (USENIX Security 2025) achieves ~90% attack success by injecting **five malicious texts per target question** into a database of millions, and finds tested defences insufficient [44]; a 13-attack / 7-defence / 5-dataset benchmark concludes "current defense techniques fail to provide robust protection" [45]. Two scope conditions the popular retelling drops. First, **the threat model requires the attacker to know the target question in advance** — this is a targeted attack against anticipated queries, not a demonstration that a corpus can be generally degraded at five-document cost. Second, the widely-repeated figures "layered defences reduce non-adaptive success from 79% to 1%, adaptive attacks reach 62%" **trace to no locatable source** and should not be repeated (see below).

Calibrate accordingly: the demonstrated exposure is an adversary who can guess or observe your queries *and* write to your index. Where nobody outside the trust boundary can write, this is a much smaller risk than the literature's tone implies — and the control is **write-path** (who may add content, provenance recorded at ingest, per-source admission policy), with read-path filtering as a supplement.

The only defence with a formal guarantee is **isolate-then-aggregate**: RobustRAG partitions retrieved passages into disjoint groups, generates independently per group, then securely aggregates, certifying "non-trivial lower bounds on response quality ... even against an adaptive attacker with full knowledge of the defense" [46]. Treat as an option, not a requirement: still no venue ~2 years after posting, 3 datasets / 3 LLMs, and certification is query-conditional. It nonetheless constrains an API shape decided early — passages must be exposable as k independently-groupable units with stable identity, i.e. return *partitioned evidence groups*, not one concatenated blob. Unresolved: whether that certification survives a corpus with genuine, non-adversarial 50/50 disagreement, which violates its implicit "most groups are clean" assumption.

### Bi-temporal modelling: what it represents, and what it structurally cannot

SQL:2011 standardises **two independent, orthogonal period mechanisms**, not one "bitemporal" feature †[47]. Application time is user-controlled (`PERIOD FOR p (start,end)`, closed-open, `UPDATE/DELETE ... FOR PORTION OF` which auto-splits overlapping rows, `PRIMARY KEY (k, p WITHOUT OVERLAPS)`, period-qualified foreign keys). System time is system-maintained (`GENERATED ALWAYS AS ROW START/END`, `WITH SYSTEM VERSIONING`, queried via `FOR SYSTEM_TIME AS OF`). A "bitemporal table" is simply both on one table; the standard never defines the term. **Critically: SQL:2011 adds no period data type** — a period is metadata over two ordinary, named, user-visible columns. The vocabulary (valid time vs transaction time) predates it †[48].

The standard enforces a deliberate **asymmetry**: on system-versioned tables users may not set or modify the system columns, may not update or delete historical rows, and constraints are enforced only on current rows; on application-time tables the user supplies and may rewrite the period, and every constraint must be interval-aware †[47]. Two integrity regimes: "what we recorded, when" is append-only and cheap; "what was true, when" is editable and expensive — and is the part you build yourself if the engine lacks it.

| Engine | Transaction time | Valid time | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| PostgreSQL 18 (2025) | no | constraints only | `WITHOUT OVERLAPS` and `PERIOD` FKs; **no** application-time period tables, no `FOR PORTION OF`, no system versioning †[49] |
| MariaDB | yes | yes | but forbids `DELETE/UPDATE FOR PORTION` on a table that also has system time †[50] |
| Datomic | yes | **no** | `(E,A,V,Tx,op)` + `:db/txInstant`; "change is represented by accumulating the new, not by modifying or removing the old" †[51] |
| XTDB | yes | yes | bitemporal by default, four auto-maintained temporal columns, "inspired by SQL:2011" †[52] |

**The decisive negative, and the pivot of this whole section: neither bi-temporal databases nor slowly changing dimensions can represent live disagreement at all.** `WITHOUT OVERLAPS` and the single-current-row rule together guarantee **exactly one row per key at any (application-time, system-time) coordinate**. A "correction" is a later transaction-time assertion superseding an earlier one — that is *latest-wins with an audit trail*. Two contemporaneous competing live claims are a **constraint violation**, not a state †[47][53]. Kimball's SCD taxonomy is a set of eight lossy choices about change over time; Types 6 and 7 are the closest analogue (dual keys / embedded current-and-historical attributes so a fact can be grouped by either the as-was or the as-is value) and are the right *shape* to copy for dual addressability — but they model change at different times, never disagreement at one time †[54][55].

So: **live disagreement is not a temporal feature.** It requires a third dimension — the claimant — that the temporal standard does not provide. Budget it as a separate axis with its own keys.

One inherited design lesson is worth taking literally. TSQL2's standardisation attempt failed and SQL/Temporal Part 7 was cancelled in 2001, because hidden/implicit period columns are incompatible with SQL's rule that all row information be captured as column values, and because "statement modifiers" that silently change query semantics "contained no clear rules specifying the semantics" †[47]; Darwen & Date reach the same conclusion from relational theory — a table with hidden attributes "is not a relation" and "clearly violates The Information Principle" †[53]. **Do not build a temporal mode that rewrites ordinary queries, and do not hide the temporal columns.** Every validity and status attribute should be nameable, selectable and joinable like any other field.

### Belief revision, TMS, paraconsistency: what to borrow and what to leave

| Tradition | Verdict for kc | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| **AGM revision** [56][57] | **Wrong architecture, by construction.** The Success postulate forces the new input to be believed and Consistency forces a consistent result — AGM is *definitionally* a winner-picking operator. It also assumes logically closed (infinite) belief sets that "resource-bounded agents cannot implement", covers single-shot revision only, and its Recovery postulate is contested | Strong (theory) † |
| Katsuno–Mendelzon **revision vs update** [58] | Borrow this one distinction: new information about a static world vs a world that changed maps exactly onto transaction time vs valid time. It is the cleanest justification for two time axes | Strong (theory) † |
| **Non-prioritized** revision: semi-revision, screened/shielded operators, belief *bases*, symmetric merging where "neither dominates" [57][59] | Borrow: an incoming claim is **not** automatically believed, merging treats sources symmetrically, and you store asserted claims — never their deductive closure | Moderate † |
| **ATMS** [60] (vs Doyle's JTMS [61]) | Closest existing engineering pattern for *keeping* contradictions: label each datum with the assumption-sets under which it holds, so mutually inconsistent contexts coexist and are queried simultaneously | Moderate; **no modern systems evaluation found** † |
| **Provenance semirings** [62] | The formally-grounded modern version: an algebra for propagating "which source-set supports this" through relational query evaluation. If disagreement must survive joins and aggregation rather than existing only at leaf records, this is the mechanism with a proven algebra | Moderate (theory strong, deployment unmeasured) † |
| **Abstract argumentation** [63] | Attack relations + extension semantics decide what is collectively acceptable *without deleting the defeated* — the defeasible-reasoning analogue of the same idea | Moderate (theory) † |
| **Paraconsistent logic** [64][65] | Adopt the four-state **vocabulary** (asserted / denied / contested / unknown) — cheap, and it names the states you need. Do **not** adopt the inference machinery: every located application is philosophical, expert-system or theoretical, with **no** benchmark or controlled comparison showing a paraconsistent store beating a resolved one downstream | **Weak** — vocabulary only † |

The one genuinely actionable formal result: querying an inconsistent store for what all consistent readings agree on is **not** prohibitively expensive for the query shapes a knowledge layer issues. For self-join-free Boolean conjunctive queries under primary keys, `CERTAINTY(q)` is either in symmetric stratified Datalog (hence LOGSPACE, frequently first-order rewritable) or coNP-complete — **nothing in between** — and "queries involving foreign-to-primary key matches, the most typical join condition, fall on the logspace side" †[66][67]. Path queries with self-joins give a tetrachotomy, and which case applies is decidable in polynomial time in query size †[68]. The framework is Arenas, Bertossi & Chomicki's: a repair is a maximal consistent subinstance, a consistent answer holds in every repair †[69]. Concretely: a `certain` query mode can sit alongside a `best-rank` mode, with queries classified **offline at schema-design time** and coNP-side queries rejected or degraded rather than run.

### Is there a tested representation that keeps live disagreement first-class?

**Representation: yes, one, deployed at scale.** Wikidata's statement model is the only field-tested design in this space †[70][71]. An item carries many statements per property; each statement carries qualifiers (point in time, start/end time, determination method), **its own references**, and a **rank** from three values — *preferred* ("the most current statement or statements that best represent consensus"), *normal* (the default, carrying "no judgement or evaluation of a value's accuracy and currency"), and *deprecated* (statements "known to include errors ... or that represent outdated knowledge"). Query semantics is **best rank**: preferred if any exist, else normal; deprecated excluded by default but retrievable. Deprecated statements are **retained, not deleted**, specifically so the error is not re-added by someone else and so the evolution of knowledge is documented — and all statements, deprecated included, must remain verifiable.

That is the reference architecture, and it answers the expiry question directly: **retain + mark + exclude by default + filter before ranking**, not delete.

The plumbing for the surrounding vocabulary is standardised; the *status* vocabulary is not. PROV-O has been a W3C Recommendation since 2013 and gives you `prov:Entity` / `prov:Activity` / `prov:Agent` plus `wasGeneratedBy` / `wasDerivedFrom` / `wasAttributedTo`, explicitly designed to be extended — do not invent a provenance vocabulary [72]. RDF 1.2 (CR snapshot, April 2026) adds triple terms reachable via `rdf:reifies`, where "a reifier may denote ... a statement or belief that the proposition holds" — i.e. you can say *Bob claims X* **without asserting X**, which is precisely the primitive live disagreement needs †[73]; named graphs were the prior mechanism †[74] and nanopublications package assertion + provenance + publication-info as one citable unit †[75]. Temporal RDF (TKDE 2007) established labelling triples with valid-time intervals and the associated entailment and query semantics, and is the right prior art for the time half of the vocabulary †[76]. What none of these standardise is the conflict relation itself (contradicts / supersedes / restates / scopes-differently) or the status/rank vocabulary — those are where custom terms are justified, and Wikidata's three ranks are the only field-tested option. Note they have never been ablated against a finer or coarser vocabulary: "three ranks" is convention, not an evidenced optimum.

**Evidence that it beats latest-wins downstream: no. Not at the store level.** This is the section's central honest finding, and it must not be softened. The available evidence splits into three piles, none of which is that result:

1. **Annotation-level (strong, but a learning-target result, not a retrieval-store result).** Modelling each annotator as a separate task gives performance "equivalent or superior" to aggregated-label training across seven tasks, with uncertainty estimates that "correlate better with annotation disagreements" †[77]; +22% on predicting individual ratings and +33% on predicting annotator variance †[78]; soft-label training "matched or exceeded hard-label accuracy on every dataset", cut KL divergence to the annotator distribution by 32%, and produced per-sample entropy correlating 61% more strongly with annotator entropy †[79]; CrowdTruth's disagreement-preserving aggregation beat majority vote on four heterogeneous tasks †[80]; a crowd-distribution signal drives abstention competitively with selective-prediction baselines †[81]; and there is a three-edition shared task with public data and metrics †[82]. **The win here is in calibration and abstention, not headline accuracy — do not promise accuracy gains.**
2. **Negative (moderate–strong).** Unmanaged conflicting and outdated content in a retrieval pool measurably *hurts* downstream accuracy even when the correct content is present †[21][22], which argues against an undifferentiated keep-everything pool but not for any particular structured alternative.
3. **One near-direct test (weak).** NuggetIndex stores atomic units as managed records, each with "links to evidence, a temporal validity interval, and a lifecycle state", filtering invalid or deprecated records **before** ranking; it reports +42% nugget recall, +9pp temporal correctness "without the recall collapse observed in time-filtered baselines", a 55% reduction in conflict rate and 64% shorter generator input †[83]. Single preprint, self-reported numbers, partly self-constructed datasets, no independent replication. Its most useful contribution is the negative one: **naive date-cutoff filtering causes recall collapse**, so the lifecycle state must be richer than a date.

And latest-wins is a **hard baseline**, not a straw man. In temporal knowledge graphs a training-free baseline that simply predicts recurring facts "requires little hyperparameter tuning and no iterative training" and ranks first or third against 11 published methods on three of five standard datasets †[84]; TGB 2.0 (8 datasets, up to 53M edges) finds "simple heuristic approaches often rival complex methods" and that scalability, not modelling, binds †[85]. Implement "most recently asserted" and "most recurrent" as first-class baselines and make them the bar before building anything.

### The design answer

**Model conflict explicitly in the representation; collapse it in the default view; build no inference machinery.** Each half of that is separately supported.

| Decision | Call | Why |
|---|---|---|
| N claims per (entity, property), each with its own source, time qualifiers and status | **Do it** | The only deployed representation of live disagreement works this way †[70]; bitemporal tables structurally cannot hold it †[47]; it is cheap and reversible |
| Default query view resolves to one answer (best-rank / latest-wins), raw view does not | **Do it** | Latest-wins is a hard baseline †[84][85]; Wikidata's best-rank semantics is the field-tested shape †[70] |
| Validity interval + lifecycle state per **assertion**, filtered **before** ranking | **Do it** | Per-fact duration beats uniform TTL [24]; relevance prefers stale [18][19]; coexisting outdated content degrades output even when current content is present †[21] |
| Retain deprecated content, exclude by default, never delete | **Do it** | Wikidata's documented rationale †[70]; naive date cutoffs collapse recall †[83] |
| Numeric per-source trust field consumed by ranker/decoder | **Do it** | Weighting and training work; prompt annotation does not [30][31][33] |
| Deduplicate; count distinct sources; cap per source and per side | **Do it** | Repetition reverses credibility preference [33]; N documents per side is an implicit vote-by-volume |
| Reuse PROV-O field names; define only the conflict/status terms | **Do it** | Stable 13-year standard, designed for extension [72] |
| Four-state status vocabulary (asserted / denied / contested / unknown) | **Do it** | Cheap; names the states — but adopt the vocabulary only †[65] |
| Paraconsistent inference, AGM revision operators, learned truth-discovery scorer in v1 | **Do not** | No evaluation showing any of them pays off †[65][57][36][37] |
| Semiring/ATMS annotation propagating support-sets through joins | **Defer behind a seam** | Right mechanism if disagreement must survive aggregation, but no modern systems evaluation †[62][60] |
| Measure the **base rate of conflict on your own corpus** before freezing the schema | **Do this first** | Highest-value action in the review: if genuine inter-context conflict is 0.1% of queries this is over-engineering; if 10%, load-bearing. Cheap: NLI over top-k on a query sample |

**What to measure.** Do not report raw RAG accuracy. Stratify by *(sufficient context, conflicting context, neither)* and report selective accuracy and coverage, otherwise a retrieval improvement and an abstention improvement are indistinguishable in the metric [35]. Add "rate at which a correct prior is overridden by a wrong retrieved claim" — accuracy alone hides it [8]. Adopt the TREC Health Misinformation judgement shape: three orthogonal judgements (usefulness, correctness, credibility) mapped to a graded ordering in which the *worst* class is useful + incorrect + **credible**, with separate helpful and harmful qrels, and compatibility rather than nDCG as the primary measure because "it is tempting to use the above scores to compute nDCG, but that ignores the incorrect information" †[86]. Its second task — total recall of misinformation in the collection — is the right internal QA measure for *how much of the disagreement your store is even aware of*. For a dashboard number ("how much disagreement is in the store"), prefer a cheap structural measure — count of entities with more than one non-deprecated competing claim, max spread per property — over a logic-based inconsistency measure, whose complexity is itself the subject of a research literature †[87] (weakly sourced: bibliographic record only). Finally, treat every conflict threshold as a per-deployment calibration parameter: an audit of eight automatic attribution scorers found per-dataset rankings **invert**, with one NLI scorer dropping from 0.90 AUROC on short-form text to 0.53 — chance — on long-form [88].

### What the evidence does not support

**"Bitemporal modelling lets you represent disagreement — you keep both versions, so both viewpoints survive."** False as stated, and structurally so. SQL:2011's `WITHOUT OVERLAPS` on application-time primary keys and the single-current-row rule on system-versioned tables both guarantee exactly one row per key at any (application-time, system-time) coordinate; a later transaction-time row *supersedes* the earlier one. That is latest-wins with an audit trail. Two live, contemporaneous, competing assertions are a **constraint violation** in the model, not a state it can hold †[47][53]. Slowly changing dimensions have the same limit: they model change over time, never disagreement at a time †[54].

**"SQL:2011 gave the industry a portable temporal database, so temporal modelling is solved."** Substantially overstated. The standard's own authors list period joins, period aggregates, period set operations, period coalescing, multiple application-time periods per table and non-temporal periods as *future* "Language Opportunities", and there is no period data type at all †[47]. Fourteen years on, adoption is thin: PostgreSQL 18 shipped only the constraints, MariaDB forbids `FOR PORTION OF` on bitemporal tables, Datomic has no valid time †[49][50][51].

**"TSQL2 / the Snodgrass approach is the canonical temporal model the standard adopted."** The opposite. The TSQL2-based ISO submission generated "considerable controversy", failed to gain support, and SQL/Temporal Part 7 was cancelled outright in 2001; SQL:2011 deliberately took a different approach because hidden/implicit period columns are incompatible with SQL's requirement that all row information be captured as column values †[47][53].

**"AGM belief revision is the theory you want for conflicting knowledge."** AGM is a theory of *eliminating* conflict by construction — Success forces the input to be believed, Consistency forces a consistent outcome — and is unimplementable as specified (infinite deductively-closed belief sets), single-shot only, with a contested Recovery postulate †[56][57]. Only the non-prioritized fringe is relevant.

**"Paraconsistent logic is the proven way to build a contradiction-tolerant database."** Unsupported by any located evaluation. The formal systems are mature and Belnap's motivation is explicit and old, but every cited application is philosophical, theoretical, or expert-system anecdote; **no benchmark, field study or controlled comparison** was found in which a paraconsistent store outperformed a repaired or resolved one on a downstream task †[64][65].

**"Truth discovery / source-reliability weighting solves multi-source conflict."** Not supported. The first systematic comparison of the 12 leading algorithms exists precisely because none had been done †[36]; on two Deep Web domains chosen because they were believed clean, the authors found large inconsistency and low source accuracy and answered their own titular question ("is the problem solved?") in the negative †[37]; at web scale, extractor noise is a qualitatively different error source from the source-conflict noise the methods were designed for †[38].

**"Modelling per-source perspectives always beats aggregating to a single answer."** Not always, and the robust win is narrower than usually claimed. On implicit discourse relation recognition, perspectivist models *underperform* unless ambiguity decreases †[89]; a SemEval-2023 perspectivist system underperformed on distributional metrics, its authors arguing the metrics need re-evaluation rather than that the approach won †[90]. The consistently replicated result is the weaker one: **keep the distribution** (soft labels) — matching or exceeding hard-label accuracy while substantially improving calibration †[79][77]. "Keep the distribution" is supported; "keep every source as a separate model" is not.

**"A cleaner index is monotonically better: filter out low-relevance documents and accuracy rises."** The most important counter-result in this literature. Cuconasu et al. find that "adding random documents in the prompt improves the LLM accuracy by up to 35%", while "the retriever's highest-scoring documents that are not directly relevant to the query negatively impact the effectiveness of the LLM" [91]. The dangerous documents are the retriever's **semantic near-misses**, not the obviously irrelevant ones — so a similarity-threshold filter removes the helpful noise and keeps the harmful distractors, and can move accuracy the wrong way. Nothing in the literature reconciles this with the poisoning literature's call for aggressive read-path filtering. **Admission control on provenance (who wrote it) is defensible; admission control on relevance is not**, and any relevance threshold must be A/B'd, never assumed beneficial.

**"Reordering passages so the trusted or freshest document sits at a favoured position mitigates conflict (the 'lost in the middle' fix)."** Under realistic retrieval, no. Over 60% of queries have at least one highly distracting passage in the top-10; positional bias "often reported as very prominent by related works, is actually marginal in real scenarios" because relevant and distracting passages are penalised alike; and "sophisticated strategies that attempt to rearrange the passages based on LLM positional preferences do not perform better than random shuffling" [34]. Order *does* flip answers under conflict — 11.4%–25.2% of biomedical predictions flip on order reversal [16] — but that is an instability to eliminate by filtering and aggregation, not a lever to tune.

**"Model confidence will tell you when retrieved evidence is contradictory, so gate on logprobs."** Contradictions "affect model confidence only marginally" [9], and what confidence tracks is the model's own prior strength, not the internal consistency of the evidence set [8]. But the strong form of this warning also overshoots: the best published result on the question *fuses* confidence with an evidence-side conflict score for 7.2–33.4 points of selective accuracy [16]. The correct statement is **"confidence is insufficient alone", not "confidence is uninformative"**.

**"Labelling sources by credibility in the prompt makes the model prefer the trustworthy one."** Preference exists but is not robust: across 13 open-weight models it "can be reversed by simply repeating information from less credible sources" [33]. What demonstrably works applies credibility as a computed attention weight [30] or a training signal [31] — not as textual annotation the model is asked to reason over.

**"Telling the model to abstain or say 'sources disagree' is a sufficient safeguard."** Consistently contradicted. Large models "often output incorrect answers instead of abstaining when the context is not [sufficient]" [35], and models identify *that* a conflict exists yet fail to pinpoint it or produce distinct viewpoints [10]. (A single-author preprint reporting that small frozen models still answer 41.6% of questions containing planted incorrect information under abstention prompting is **suggestive only** — 3.8B–8B models, unreplicated [92].) Abstention must be driven by a computed sufficiency/conflict signal, not requested in the system prompt.

**"Corpus poisoning requires controlling a meaningful share of the corpus, so a large index is inherently safe."** False, but narrower than usually retold: five malicious texts per target question against a database of millions gives ~90% success **because the attack is targeted — the attacker optimises for the query, not for corpus share** — and defences fail across a 13-attack / 7-defence benchmark [44][45]. The omitted scope condition is that PoisonedRAG's threat model **requires advance knowledge of the target question**.

**"Semantic retrieval will naturally surface the current version of a fact."** The opposite bias is measured: re-rankers show "a strong bias toward older, semantically rich documents, even when they are factually obsolete" [18], models "tend to return outdated answers when retrieved documents do not provide sufficient information" [20], and accuracy drops up to 31% on evolving-knowledge questions over real timestamped corpora [19]. Caveat: the sharpest version of this result and its proposed fix come from the same industry lab.

**"RAG grounds the model and therefore reduces hallucination."** Only conditionally, and one peer-reviewed source implies it can invert: Astute RAG reports its method is "the only RAG method that achieves performance comparable to or even surpassing conventional use of LLMs under the worst-case scenario" — i.e. ordinary RAG falls *below* no-retrieval when retrieval is bad [15]. Add that models favour fluent generated context over fragmented retrieved context even when the generated context is wrong [13]. "RAG reduces hallucination" is a statement about the average case over a clean corpus, not a safety property.

**"Querying an inconsistent store for what all consistent readings agree on is too expensive to be practical."** False for the queries that matter — LOGSPACE / often first-order-rewritable for self-join-free conjunctive queries under primary keys, with foreign-to-primary key joins (the most typical join condition) on the cheap side, and the classification decidable in polynomial time in query size †[66][67][68].

**"Superseded content should be deleted."** The largest deployed system doing this at scale does the opposite and documents why: deprecated statements are retained — excluded from the default best-rank view but still queryable — so the erroneous value is not re-added by someone else and so the evolution of knowledge is documented †[70].

**"There is an established result showing that conflict-preserving representations beat latest-wins downstream."** **No such result was found.** The honest position for a design document is that keeping disagreement is a well-motivated but **empirically unvalidated architecture at the store level**, and the build should be instrumented to produce the missing evidence itself †[83][79][21].

**"The prevalence of conflict in a real corpus is a known quantity you can design against."** No source in this literature measures it. WikiContradict yields 253 human-annotated instances from the whole of Wikipedia [11]; ConflictBank's 7.4M pairs are synthetic [3]; WhoQA's 5,000 questions are constructed by design from same-name entity collisions [12]; Gokul et al. explicitly *generate* simulated contradictions rather than observe them [4]; Astute RAG asserts imperfect retrieval is "inevitable, common, and harmful" with **no figure** [15]. Every prevalence claim in the field is an assertion. Anyone building to them is designing against an unmeasured base rate.

**Provenance failures — claims traceable to no primary source.**

- **"Layered defences reduce non-adaptive poisoning success from 79% to 1%, and adaptive attacks exploiting document formatting reach 62%."** These figures circulated in an earlier draft of this review attributed to three specific papers. **None of those abstracts contains the figures and no source for them could be located.** Treat as unsupported and do not repeat.
- **"Truth-discovery algorithms are highly sensitive to initialisation, parameter setting and convergence behaviour."** The cited source states only that it *studies* "the impact of initialization and parameter setting, convergence, and scalability" — aspects "that have not been explicitly studied before". **It states no sensitivity verdict at abstract level**, and it is a QCRI technical report, not a peer-reviewed benchmark [36]. Note the two source reviews **disagree** on this exact point: the unverified bi-temporal review repeats the sensitivity claim as established; the adversarially-verified knowledge-conflict review removes it. Making initialisation and parameters configurable is good practice regardless — it is just not backed by the source usually cited for it.
- **Three widely-repeated venue attributions could not be confirmed on the arXiv abstract pages at verification time**: "Sufficient Context" as ICLR 2025 [35], "RealTime QA" as NeurIPS 2023 Datasets & Benchmarks [20], and "The Power of Noise" as SIGIR 2024 [91]. All three are cited above; all three should be treated as preprints until independently confirmed. The two survey papers supplying the field's canonical conflict taxonomy are likewise preprints with no journal-ref — **the taxonomy's authority comes from adoption, not from peer review** [1][2].
- **"Post-filtering access control silently collapses recall."** Plausible engineering reasoning that is routinely presented as a literature finding. **No cited paper measures post-filter recall collapse**; policy-aware vector search is merely *framed* as an open problem balancing enforcement, ANN recall and latency. Treat the pre-filter-vs-post-filter choice as an untested hypothesis to validate on your own corpus.
- **"Surfacing disagreement to the user improves their decisions."** WhoQA *recommends* that models "transparently inform users about the conflicts rather than autonomously deciding what to present" [12], and every evaluation located in this literature is model-facing. **No user study was found** testing whether "sources disagree, here are both" produces better human outcomes than a single confident answer — or merely produces abandonment. If the product's answer to conflict is to escalate to the user, that is an unevidenced bet.

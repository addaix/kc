# Research detail: why knowledge systems fail

Supporting detail for [../research.md](../research.md). Claims are graded strong / moderate / weak / contested.

## Why knowledge systems fail

The failure literature is the most durable part of this corpus and the part that most directly constrains what `kc` should be. It is also where the marketing case for this entire product category is weakest: three of the four numbers usually deployed to justify building a knowledge system either misquote their source or have no primary source at all. What survives scrutiny is not a failure *rate* but a set of failure *mechanisms* — reproducible, named, and mostly independent of technology generation. Those mechanisms, not the statistics, are what a design can be built against.

**Evidence grades** used below: **strong** (multiple independent studies, or one large well-identified study with verified primary text), **moderate** (single study, single site, relayed quotation, or a well-argued but unmeasured claim), **weak** (anecdote, position paper, self-described pilot, or a claim resting on a single unreplicated source), **contested** (competent evidence pointing both ways).

**†** marks any finding drawn from the transactive-memory topic review — the only one of the six source reviews that did **not** receive an adversarial verification pass. Directions from it are usable; magnitudes and quotations should be treated as unchecked, and I state them more weakly than the source does.

### The base rate does not exist, and the field's canonical statistics do not survive tracing

Every pitch for a knowledge system opens with a number. None of the four in common use holds up.

| Claim in circulation | Traces to | What the primary actually says | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| "84% of KM programmes fail / show no considerable impact" | Lucier & Torsilieri (1997), a Booz Allen consulting article [1] | "about one-sixth of these programs achieve very significant impact within the first two years; half achieve small but important benefits; and the remaining third — the failures — have little business impact." Their own failure figure is **one third**. It is prefaced "We estimate", based on "our five years of involvement… and in discussions with participants in more than 70 leading programs" — no sampling frame, no impact criterion | **Misquoted.** 84% = 1 − one-sixth, which silently reclassifies the half that achieved "small but important benefits" as failures |
| "70% of KM (or change, or digital) initiatives fail" | Five prominent secondary sources | Hughes reviewed all five and found in every case an absence of valid and reliable empirical evidence — each states the number without support or cites another source that does [2] | **Unsupported** |
| "Knowledge workers spend ~2.5 hours/day (30% of the workday) searching" | IDC white paper, July 2001, doc 01C2996TELECO2996, marked "Sponsored by Inktomi" — a search vendor [3] | Verbatim, under a heading titled *Assumptions*: "We use a general estimate that the typical knowledge worker spends about 2.5 hours per day, or roughly 30% of the workday, searching for information… IDC believes the number represents a general average." No survey, sample or instrumentation is reported. The paper also says "any calculation of cost must necessarily be imprecise". Its own Scenario 1 gives $2.5M/1,000 workers, not the $5M usually quoted | **An assumption, cited as a measurement** |
| "80–90% of organisational knowledge is tacit / in employees' heads" | See the tacit-knowledge subsection below | No primary source locatable; the chains terminate in trade magazines and a dead marketing microsite | **No primary source** |

Two independent verified reviews reached the first and second of these separately, which is itself the strongest signal in the table.

The deeper point is that the failure-rate question may not be answerable. Eveleens & Verhoef applied the canonical IT-failure taxonomy (the Standish CHAOS definitions) to 5,457 forecasts of 1,211 real projects and found the definitions "misleading, one-sided, pervert the estimation practice, and result in meaningless figures"; their Table 2 reports success rates of 67%, 5.8% and 59% across three organisations driven purely by differences in *estimation bias*, not by project outcomes [4] (**strong**). Note also that Standish's 1994 figures (16% / 53% / 31%) are numerically near-identical to Lucier & Torsilieri's 1997 "estimate" (one-sixth / half / one-third) — no derivation is asserted here, only that a KM number indistinguishable from a contemporaneous, later-discredited software statistic is not independent corroboration. The academic KM failure literature cannot close the gap either: the multi-case studies code failure factors from *other people's published cases*, and Akhavan & Pezeshkan concede in their own abstract that "organizations do not provide accurate reports of failure in their projects because of their policies, the image of their organization, and privacy" [5] (**strong**, as a statement about the evidence base).

Design consequence: do not put a failure rate in the design document and do not let one motivate the design. Anchor on mechanisms.

### Contribution: the disparity is real, the folk explanations of it are not

The single most transferable mechanism is Grudin's disparity, verified word-for-word in the CSCW '88 full text: "The application fails because it requires that some people do additional work, while those people are not the ones who perceive a direct benefit from the use of the application", compounded by a design failure ("decision-makers see the potential benefits for people similar to themselves, but don't see the implications of the fact that extra work will be required of others") and an evaluation failure [6] (**strong**).

Two scope corrections matter and are usually dropped. First, Grudin himself scoped the "discretion probably won't work" corollary: "Not all CSCW applications introduce such a disparity — with electronic mail, for example, everyone generally shares the benefits and burdens equally", and the argument's stated premise is that "Communication, at the heart of most CSCW applications, will break down without relatively uniform use" [6] (**moderate** as a general law). A store whose value accrues *per item* — one contribution useful to one reader regardless of whether anyone else contributes — is outside the argument as Grudin made it. Second, the disparity has never been tested as a quantitative predictor of adoption; Duckert & Bjørn's 2024 two-site revisit recategorises Grudin's challenges rather than retiring them, and reports groupware now "seamlessly integrated into organizations, considered essential infrastructure" [7][8] (**moderate**).

The knowledge-sharing dilemma is a genuine public-goods structure — Cabrera & Cabrera, verified verbatim: "withholding from cooperating yields the best individual utility, regardless of what everyone else in the group does" [9] (**moderate**; a theoretical application of the social-dilemma experiments, not a test of them). Their relayed datum that "one third of the participants refused to contribute to a discretionary database, even when the cost was minimal" comes from Connolly, Thorn & Heminger (1992) [10] and should be cited as *reported by* Cabrera & Cabrera — neither source review read the 1992 study (**weak** provenance, correct direction).

Where the literature is genuinely informative is on which levers move contribution, and here several popular ones fail their direct tests.

| Proposed lever | Best evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce friction / make capture cheap | Necessary, demonstrably insufficient. One third refused at minimal cost [9]; and Narayan et al.'s large-scale invitation-based field experiment on a gamified onboarding tutorial found "no effect of either using the tutorial or of being invited to do so over a period of 180 days" [11] | **strong** that it is insufficient |
| Extrinsic rewards | Contested. Bock et al. (N=154 managers, 27 organisations): "Contrary to common belief, we find anticipated extrinsic rewards exert a negative effect on individuals' knowledge-sharing attitudes" [12]. Kankanhalli et al. find extrinsic benefits work *contingent on* trust, pro-sharing norms and identification, while intrinsic benefits are unmoderated [13]. All measure intention or self-reported usage, never observed contribution | **contested** |
| Counter status anxiety / knowledge hoarding | Failed its most direct test. Kankanhalli et al. modelled loss of knowledge power and loss of image explicitly as costs and report: "The loss of knowledge power and image do not appear to impact EKR usage by knowledge contributors" [13] — against vivid field quotes from Orlikowski's up-or-out consultancy ("if you put all this information in a Notes database you lose power") [8] | **contested**; the mechanism appears contingent on reward structure, not general |
| Visible attribution / reputation | Positively evidenced. Wasko & Faraj: people contribute "when they perceive that it enhances their professional reputations… Surprisingly, contributions occur without regard to expectations of reciprocity" [14]. Constant, Sproull & Kiesler found strangers give useful advice without personal connection, "positive regard for the larger organization can substitute for direct incentives" [15] | **moderate** (correlational, partly self-report) |
| Feedback that a contribution was used | Half-supported. Cabrera & Cabrera recommend it — "establish mechanisms by which employees receive feedback whenever others use their contributions" — and immediately warn that "negative feedback might reduce an employee's information self-efficacy and, consequently, reduce the likelihood that the employee will choose to contribute" [9]. Neither direction is tested | **weak** |
| A human who responds | The sharpest experimental pair available. Same community, opposite outcomes: the gamified tutorial produced nothing [11], while the Teahouse — a *staffed human help space* — measurably improved retention "for both low- and high-activity newcomers, and for both short- and long-term survival" [16] | **moderate**; two studies, one community, not designed as a comparison |

The most useful reframing is that "culture" dissolves into legible accounting. At Orlikowski's Alpha the binding constraints were billable-hours accounting ("We don't have the time to read or enter information in Notes. What would I charge it to?") and an up-or-out tournament, all quotable from informants [8] (**moderate**: one office, five months, early rollout; Orlikowski herself declines the general verdict and her own later two-year study documents the opposite trajectory [17]). The checkable design question is therefore: what is the contributor's accounting unit, and does contribution count against it?

### Maintenance, not capture, is what kills the artifact

The failure that recurs across independent systems is unstaffed upkeep.

Grudin & Poole's enterprise-wiki study is the clearest record, and its quotations are verified verbatim: the Orbit team wiki was "going to weed" after a major contributor left and the assigned replacement was reassigned — "with no 'gardener,' its appearance and consistency suffered"; and, decisively, "No one we interviewed mentioned 'pruning stale content' as an activity that they engaged in" [18] (**moderate** — the widely-quoted "66% had not been used in the past six months" came from a survey to over 4,000 users of a platform *being decommissioned*, with an ~11% response rate; do not quote it as a base rate for wikis).

Markus's synthesis names the same thing and its remedy: "repositories often require considerable rework to be useful for new reusers, but knowledge producers rarely have the resources and incentives to do a good job of repurposing knowledge. Solutions include careful use of incentives and human and technical intermediaries" [19] (**strong** at abstract level; her case-level detail was not independently verified by either review and should be re-checked before external use). Her four reuser types — shared-work producers, shared-work practitioners, expertise-seeking novices, secondary knowledge miners — have incompatible requirements: producers and practitioners want *contextualised* records, novices need *decontextualised* knowledge plus guidance on what context matters. One undifferentiated store cannot serve both.

The design-rationale tradition tells the same story, and here the usual retelling is wrong in an instructive way. The source sentence reads in full: "The methods resulted in **long-term cost reductions**, but success relied on severe social pressure, extensive training, or continuing human facilitation." Quoting only the second clause converts "works but is expensive to sustain" into "fails" [20] (**moderate**; the paper describes its own evidence as "anecdotal accounts of our own experiences… as well as corroborative reports by others"). Compendium's own retrospective makes the staffing dependence explicit: earlier tools "did not receive sustained use due to issues of cognitive overheads and representational inflexibility", and what changed the outcome was "the impact that a hypertext facilitator can have" [21] (**moderate**; a two-page conference position paper, not an evaluation).

Content decay is measured, and here the two adversarially-verified reviews **disagree with each other**, which is worth recording rather than smoothing. Both agree, from Zhang et al.'s mining study of Stack Overflow, that "more than half of the obsolete answers (58.4 percent) were probably already obsolete when they were first posted" [22] (**strong**) — most rot is *born*, not acquired, so age alone is a weak staleness proxy. They disagree on repair: one review reports 20.5% of observed-obsolete answers ever updated; the other, reading the full text, reports that figure as coming from a 669-answer qualitative subsample, with full-corpus **upper bounds** of 27.4% updated, 33.3% given a new answer and 49.8% either. Both agree the repair is slow (227 days to a first update) and that obsolescence is most often noticed by **outsiders with no prior involvement** (38.2%) rather than by the answerer (24.3%). Treat the repair rate as somewhere between one fifth and one half, conditional on the staleness having already been noticed, and treat the detector-identity finding as the design-relevant one.

Finally, volume is not a proxy for retained knowledge: Argote, Beckman & Epple, across multiple production organisations, found that "knowledge acquired through production depreciates rapidly. The conventional measure of learning, cumulative output, significantly overstates the persistence of learning" [23] (**strong**). Cumulative index size is the exact analogue and inherits the overstatement.

### The social-technical gap: a durable property, not an engineering backlog

Ackerman's statement of the gap is "the great divide between what we know we must support socially and what we can support technically… Simply put, we do not know how to build systems that fully support the social world uncovered by these findings" [24] (**strong**, though note an access asymmetry between the two source reviews: one retrieved and verified the full text and all quotations; the other could reach only Crossref metadata, four open mirrors being dead). He names three representational failures — insufficient nuance, insufficient social flexibility (fluid implicit role-switching versus required explicit state switches), insufficient ambiguity ("people do not inhabit the discrete states of a decision or action graph") — and three instances that bear directly here: access-control models far cruder than people's disclosure behaviour; "information must be recontextualized in order to reuse experience or knowledge. Systems often assume a shared understanding of information"; and "Systems do not allow sufficient ambiguity."

He also pre-rejects the escape route the current moment invites: one "could argue that the social-technical gap will be solved shortly by some new technology or software technique", and he shows why neither that nor the adaptation argument defeats designing around the gap [24]. Honesty requires stating the limit of this: twenty-five years without closure is an observation, not a proof, and Ackerman says as much.

His prescription is a menu of "first-order approximations", of which the strongest is architectural: "technical architectures that do not invoke the social technical gap; these architectures do not require action nor delegate it. Instead, these architectures provide supportive or augmentative facilities, such as advice… rather than trying to mechanize elements of their social environment", plus "some secondary mechanism or communication back-channel to allow users to negotiate the norms of use, exceptions, and breakdowns among themselves" [24] (**moderate** — a design argument, not an evaluation).

The most important correction to the usual framing comes from what neither the popular reading nor the prior reviews engaged: Schmidt & Bannon's position that articulation work is *irreducible* — a permanent overhead of distributed cooperative work whose design task is to **support** it, not to eliminate its cost [25] (**moderate**; foundational theory paper, not read in full by the source review). On that framing, "articulation cost is the binding constraint" is a category error. Kankanhalli et al. supply the empirical version of the same point: codification effort's impact on repository contribution is *moderated* by pro-sharing norms, so removing the effort does not remove the moderator [13]. The defensible residual claim is narrow: articulation cost predicts which capture strategies get *abandoned* (dedicated-authoring surfaces do), while norms, intrinsic motivation, decontextualisation and decay predict whether a surviving strategy delivers value.

Two further constraints from this tradition. Shipman & Marshall's four causes of formalisation refusal — cognitive overhead, tacit knowledge, premature structure, situational structure — remain the best decomposition available, with the correction that they are backed by experience reports rather than measurement [20] (**moderate**, downgraded on the source's own self-description). Their remedy is the acceptance bar for any inferred structure: accept informal input, infer later, present inferences as suggestions, because "as long as the inferences are right part of the time and it is apparent to the user when the system has made the wrong inference, these features will cost the user little for the benefit they provide" — a UI requirement, not a model requirement, and one nobody has calibrated (no study establishes what error rate users tolerate before they stop trusting inferences). And Kittur & Kraut found that adding editors improved Wikipedia article quality only with appropriate coordination and was harmful without it, with *implicit* coordination (a small subset doing most of the work) helping more as editor count grew [26] (**moderate**; observational, causal direction unidentified).

### Transactive memory: does an external who-knows-what directory substitute, or degrade?

*Every claim in this subsection carries* **†** *— the transactive-memory topic review received no adversarial verification pass. Directions are usable; effect sizes and quotations are unchecked. Two of its results are independently corroborated from a verified review and are marked as such.*

The framing question is malformed in the primary source. Wegner's chapter treats the who-knows-what directory and the what-is-known store as the *same* data structure: external encoding requires a **label** and a **location**, "the item itself need not be known", and "Other people can be locations of external storage for the individual" — alongside books, files and floppy disks † [27]. The people-versus-documents split is a later empirical convention, not a claim of the theory.

On whether the directory earns its place as a distinct artifact, the meta-analytic evidence is a genuine two-step, and the second step is the decision-changing one:

| Question | Evidence | Grade |
|---|---|---|
| Do who-knows-what representations beat shared-knowledge ones? | Yes. Compilational (distributed/pointer) cognition ρ = .44 (k=26) vs compositional (shared) .32 (k=33); on objective performance .42 vs .26 † [28] | **strong** as reported |
| Is the bare directory where the effect lives? | **No.** The TMS *specialization* subscale alone — the closest proxy to pure who-knows-what differentiation — predicts at ρ = .35 overall and .29 objective, statistically indistinguishable in magnitude from shared-knowledge measures. Global TMS reaches .47 only with *credibility* and *coordination* included † [28] | **moderate** (the objective-performance cell is thin: k=5, N=318) |
| Can a directory be externalised at all? | Yes: groups given written information about members' expertise performed comparably to groups trained together; a review of 64 studies states "direct interpersonal communication is not necessary for TMS development or utilization" † [29] | **moderate**, and the load-bearing primary (Moreland & Myaskovsky [30]) was **not obtainable** by the source review — read it before betting on it |
| Does an external store *degrade* the interpersonal version? | No evidence for it, and some against: repository use and person-to-person exchange interact rather than trade off † [31]; digital repository use *weakened* the negative effect of work remoteness on accuracy of expert recognition † | **weak-to-moderate**, all cross-sectional; no longitudinal study exists |
| Does it help everywhere? | No. In hospital ward teams all three TMS subscales together explained **6%** of team-performance variance; specialization was not significant; credibility was significantly **negative** — lower reported reliance on team members' knowledge went with better performance † [32] | **moderate** (small sample, self-report, no objective performance measure; authors acknowledge both) |
| Does measurement inflate the effect? | Sharply. Self-report scales yield r = .77 against team outcomes; observer ratings .38 and embedded behavioural metrics .39 — the two behavioural instruments agree with each other and disagree with self-report [33]. **This one is corroborated from an adversarially-verified review**, which retrieved the record from Crossref directly and adds the caveat that the corpus is 44 *laboratory* studies. It is also contested: Zhou & Pazos found operationalisation did **not** moderate [34] | **contested**, but the direction should govern evaluation design |

Three further results reshape what "success" means for a store. Choi, Lee & Yoo (139 teams, 743 individuals) found knowledge *sharing* had **no direct effect** on team performance — its entire effect ran through knowledge **application** † [35]. Haas & Hansen, on 182 consulting sales proposals with an objective win/loss outcome, found highly experienced teams were **more** likely to lose bids if they obtained and used electronic documents or colleague advice † [36] — "Competitive performance depends not on how much firms know but on how they use what they know." And Bachrach et al.'s meta-analysis (76 studies, 6,869 sampling units) found informational and gender diversity **negatively** associated with TMS development † [37] — the teams whose members most need a map of who knows what are the ones least likely to form one spontaneously, which is an argument *for* building the artifact rather than waiting for emergence.

Wegner's own theory contributes one mechanism with real design teeth: a wrong or stale pointer fails **silently**. "Faulty location knowledge on either member's part dooms the system, allowing items to pass through the group without being stored"; and "When expertise is in dispute, information may fall in the cracks as well" † [27] (**moderate**; argued, not measured). A failure that raises no exception and produces only absence has to be made detectable by construction.

Finally, the cognitive-offloading literature — cited constantly in this space — has been meta-analysed, and the result cuts against the usual product reading: "the benefit of offloading is greater for **forced-** compared to **choice-**offloading conditions", and offloading "reduces interindividual variability in the performance of memory-based tasks" [38] (**moderate**; from an adversarially-verified review). Product use is a choice condition, so lab magnitudes overstate; and a store's measurable effect may be *levelling* rather than *lifting*, which predicts that mean-based A/B tests will under-detect its value. The related "reliability is the switch" finding — saving a file improved memory for the next file, but not when the saving process was deemed unreliable [39] — was **downgraded** on adversarial review: the reliability manipulation has never been independently replicated, and preregistered follow-up work finds the saving-enhanced memory effect asymmetric and order-dependent [40].

### Tacit knowledge: the load-bearing statistic has no primary source

This is the most consequential provenance finding in the whole review, because the "most organisational knowledge is tacit" claim is the premise of every pitch in this category.

Three variants circulate, and two were re-verified verbatim against the source texts:

- **90%.** Smith (2001) contains, verbatim, "Reportedly, 90 percent of the knowledge in any organization is embedded and synthesized in peoples' heads" — the hedge *Reportedly* is in the original — citing three **trade magazines** (Wah 1999; Bonner 2000; Lee 2000), none of which reports a sample, method or instrument [41]. The same article carries "Reportedly, 99 percent of the work people do is knowledge based (Wah, 1999b)", which is a tell that the source is a supplier of round rhetorical numbers.
- **42%.** Serban & Luan (2002) read verbatim: "A 2001 survey found that while '26 percent of knowledge in the average organization is stored on paper and 20 percent digitally, an astonishing 42 percent is stored in employees' heads' (Malhotra, 2001)" — whose reference entry resolves to a **British Telecom promotional microsite**, no longer live [42]. The three numbers sum to 88%; the missing 12% is never explained. This variant reads as the most credible precisely because a three-way breakdown makes a marketing page look like a survey.
- **80% ("Delphi Group").** Circulates in fixed phrasing with no report title, year, sample size, sampling frame or method, credited to a consultancy rather than a research body. Independent bibliographic search returned nothing.

The honest formulation — and the one the reviewer explicitly corrected the prior draft to — is "**no primary source is locatable despite targeted searching**", not "no primary source exists". That is sufficient, and it is a differentiator: nobody else in this category owns it.

Worse for the claim: under Polanyi's actual formulation the quantity is **undefined**. Tacit knowing is a from-to structure — attending *from* subsidiary particulars *to* a focal target — an aspect of all knowing rather than a bucket of items: "while tacit knowledge can be possessed by itself, explicit knowledge must rely on being tacitly understood and applied. Hence all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge. A wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable" [43] (**strong**). A proportion requires two disjoint countable sets; there is no such partition, so "X% of knowledge is tacit" is ill-formed regardless of X. Note the citation trap: this sentence is from Polanyi's 1966 *Philosophy* article "The Logic of Tacit Inference", **not** *The Tacit Dimension*, and citing it to the latter is itself an instance of the sloppiness documented below.

That sloppiness is measured. Grant's audit of 52 papers citing Polanyi across three KM journals found 37% clearly showed evidence of having read him, 42% unlikely to have read him, 21% indeterminate, 23% misinterpreting — "the misconceptions really affected more than 40% of the relevant papers", the most frequent being "the suggestion that Polanyi identifies two types of knowledge — tacit and explicit — and that this is an either/or state" [44] (**moderate**, downgraded: single coder, inspection only, no inter-rater reliability, author calls it "a first effort"). Hadjimichael & Tsoukas' systematic review of 171 articles across 17 leading journals puts two-thirds of the field (66%) in the *Conversion* perspective that its own critics call incoherent, against 23% *Interactional* and 11% *Practice* [45] (**strong**; explicit reproducible protocol).

On SECI specifically, the defensible statement is narrower than the anti-KM reading. Gourlay & Nurse's audit of Nonaka's *own founding evidence* finds the 1993–94 validation measured semantic information creation rather than knowledge, addressed content while SECI is a process model, and reported confirmatory-factor variance explained of 51% (externalization) and 56% (internalization) against a conventional 60–70% threshold — concluding "there is no unambiguous evidence at all for any of the four modes" while conceding "tentative support for externalization" [46] (**moderate**). But independent studies claiming to confirm all four modes exist (Dyck et al. 2005; Akbar & Tzokas 2013), unassessed by either review, and Nonaka & von Krogh explicitly reassert that conversion is explained "theoretically and empirically" [47] (**contested**). SECI's validation is inadequate; SECI has not been refuted. That is already enough reason not to build a conversion pipeline, and the stronger claim will not survive a hostile technical reviewer.

The scoping tool that actually helps is Collins' three-type taxonomy: **relational** tacit knowledge, held back or never occasioned by human relations; **somatic**, tied to bodily ability, which "even sophisticated technologies cannot adequately capture"; and **collective**, the taken-for-granted shared beliefs of a community, where "although, in principle, collective TK is convertible, any conversion would be obsolete after a while and, therefore, unhelpful" [48] (**moderate**; characterised through a systematic review, not read directly). Relational is the addressable scope; somatic is out of scope; collective is capturable but decays *by nature* — a principled reason to build expiry into the schema rather than treating staleness as an ops problem.

The citable replacement for the missing statistic is better than the number it replaces, because it names the gap instead of measuring it. Hertzum & Pejtersen's field study of engineers found they "search for documents to find people, search for people to get documents, and interact socially to get information without engaging in explicit searches", and — the sharper half — "design documentation seems to be biased toward technical aspects of the chosen solution, while information about the context of the design process is typically not available. Hence, people become a critical source of information because they can explain and argue about why specific decisions were made" [49] (**moderate**; two organisations). What is missing from documentation is **decision rationale and context**, not volume. That is a testable claim about a specific corpus and a specific thing to capture.

One further scope constraint from the same author's review of 142 studies: expertise seeking is fundamentally **re-finding** — "EXS research assumes the existence of knowledgeable sources… If a knowledgeable source does not exist then EXS becomes an inferior strategy because re-finding is no longer possible" [50] (**moderate**). A knowledge-context system is strong where an answer exists in someone's head or history and structurally weak at synthesising what nobody knows; benchmarking on novel-synthesis queries will produce misleading failure.

### Enterprise search: what actually transfers to a retrieval-plus-synthesis system

The root cause of enterprise search's underperformance is not the absence of a link graph. It is that enterprise content is **deposited, not published**. Fagin et al.'s Axiom 1, from the IBM intranet: "Intranet documents are often created for simple dissemination of information, rather than to attract and hold the attention of any specific group of users", preceded by the observation that "in intranets, there is no incentive for creating and frequently updating hubs on specific topics"; and Axiom 4: "large portions of intranet documents are not designed to be returned as answers to search queries" [51] (**strong**). Every signal web search gets free because authors compete for attention — good titles, descriptive anchors, clean URLs, accurate metadata — must be manufactured on the reader's side.

| Web-search asset | Enterprise analogue | Does it transfer? |
|---|---|---|
| Anchor text (how others refer to a thing) | Present and dominant: 87.5 "influence" at recall@20 on popular IBM queries; superior to content, title, metadata and URL words in 5 of 6 Australian organisations [51][52] | **Yes** — strongest single query-dependent signal. But "influence" is a defined quantity, not an ablation delta, and much of the supporting evidence uses *sitemap-derived* query sets that the same group showed bias toward exactly this signal. Take the direction, not the magnitude (**strong** direction, **moderate** magnitude) |
| Link authority (PageRank) | Corrected in both directions. "PageRank gives no discernable benefit over indegree for collections up to 18.5 million pages" [52]; hyperlink methods "do not seem to provide practical benefits" below 20M pages [53] — **but** the sentence immediately preceding that conclusion reads "Indegree and PageRank provide improvement if the reranking cutoff can be chosen optimally", and IBM's own tables show PageRank as the third-most-influential signal at early ranks (13.6 at R3, decaying to 2.7 at R20) while **indegree is ~0 or negative throughout** [51] | **Partly.** A cheap authority prior is defensible as an early-rank tiebreaker; an expensive one is not; indegree is worth nothing (**moderate**) |
| External/global web signals | Tested directly: no difference between Stanford's own appliance and site-restricted global Google over 1,266 navigational queries; across seven organisations 31 queries improved and 43 worsened out of 1,694 [54] | **No** — but hold loosely: crawls "were not run to completion", answer presence was largely unverified, and the query set is sitemap-derived (**moderate**) |
| Author-applied topic metadata | Refuted in the most favourable published setting: at a university with policy commitment and staff training, 43.5% of pages had no subject metadata and 50.7% of those with it repeated verbatim in 20+ other pages; metadata was "essential in 1 query, of use in 7… and of no use in 37" of 85 examined; adding subject/description metadata to a title+content baseline **degraded** performance while link counts and URL length improved it [55] | **No.** And the authors argue the problem is structural, not resourcing: metadata "cannot simultaneously be specific, unambiguous, rich, and drawn from a limited vocabulary" (**strong** on the measurement, **moderate** on the inherence argument) |
| Log-mined query suggestion closing the vocabulary gap | Unavailable — enterprise logs are too sparse, and suggestions must additionally be access-trimmed and must avoid suggesting queries with no answer [56]. The gap itself is *worse* inside organisations: one university's users expressed a single need as "results", "academic results", "grades", "marks", "transcripts", "exam results", each with 50+ occurrences [55] | **No.** Query-side synonym/alias/acronym expansion is the one hand-curated vocabulary artefact the evidence supports |

Underneath the vocabulary problem sits Furnas et al.'s measurement, still the floor: across five domains "two people favored the same term with probability <0.20… the popular approach in which access is via one designer's favorite single word will result in 80–90 percent failure rates", with the derived optimum being "unlimited aliasing" [57] (**strong**). The scope caveat matters for a modern stack: aggressive aliasing is precisely what dense retrieval already implements, so the 80% figure is the failure rate of the design this finding tells you *not* to build, not a prediction about your system.

Three results from this literature bear directly on evaluation and are the most transferable things in it.

First, **a large fraction of complaints are coverage failures, not ranking failures**: "A surprisingly high proportion of complaints about enterprise search tools actually arise because a wanted document is not even in the search engine's index" — the document doesn't exist, is out of scope ("a very common reason for failure"), is not permitted, was unreachable by the gatherer, failed text extraction, or the index was stale [58] (**moderate**; textbook distillation of deployment experience). Improving ranking is usually not the available lever: "even the best search technology cannot provide a good experience if data quality is not addressed" [56].

Second, **users do not know what they missed**. In the seminal full-text retrieval study, attorneys believed they had retrieved about 75% of the relevant documents when in fact roughly 20% had been retrieved, attributed to the inherent ambiguity of language [59][60] (**strong** as a landmark result; one legal matter, 1980s Boolean system — the mechanism transfers, the ratio does not). This qualifies the best modern satisfaction study: Cleverley & Burnett found 62% of dissatisfaction events in a multinational attributable to human information- and search-literacy factors rather than technology [61] (**moderate**; one organisation, self-selected feedback, unreplicated) — but that measures the composition of *complaints*, and Blair & Maron show that is not the composition of *failures*. A system that returns a confident synthesised answer removes the last cue — a short result list — that something was missed.

Third, **a benchmark number is uninterpretable without its judging protocol and query-sampling method**. Change the judges and system ordering changes: "the bronze judges may not be able to substitute for topic and task experts, due to changes in the relative performance of assessed systems, and gold judges are preferred" [62]; primary-versus-secondary assessors within TREC 2005 gave rankings at Kendall's τ = 0.763 [63]. Change how you pick queries and estimates move sharply, and a sitemap-derived set *reordered the engines* [64] (**moderate**; a two-page poster, one site, one non-expert judge). Change judging strictness and the best TREC 2008 expert-search run moves from MAP 0.4490 (official qrels) to 0.2950 (unanimous judging).

Two structural cautions. The enterprise-search evidence base is far less independent than it looks: roughly fourteen of the twenty-four core references trace to one connected research community (Hawking and the CSIRO/ANU/TREC group), so several findings graded "strong" on "multiple independent field experiments" are one group's programme replicated across corpora — the genuinely independent corroborations are IBM [51] and Cleverley & Burnett [61]. And the most recent primary measurement in this literature is from 2008, with the most recent authoritative synthesis 2010, restated 2017 [56] — claims of the form "X is unsolved" are assertions about the state of a literature and decay with time.

The operating-cost finding is the one that most directly predicts abandonment: "In enterprise search there is rarely more than one lonely person with the responsibility for supporting the search application", against 20,000+ engineers at a web search engine; industry surveys report 71% of organisations calling enterprise search vital or essential against 58% showing little or no search maturity, 38% never having tuned at all [56] (**moderate**; self-selected vendor-adjacent survey instruments — directionally credible, numerically soft). The failure loop it produces: complaints lead to "upgrading" the search application, and "the results from the replacement search application are usually no better."

### Distributed cognition and extended mind: a framing device, not design-bearing theory

**Stated plainly: this is a framing device.** Neither distributed cognition nor the extended mind thesis yields a design-bearing constraint, and their own authors say so.

The founding HCI statement of distributed cognition gives as its central hypothesis that "the cognitive and computational properties of systems can be accounted for in terms of the organization and propagation of constraints" — a redescription no observation could contradict — and its four core principles constrain no build. Its closing admission is decisive: "the integrated research program described in this paper does not yet exist… We will need to await the results of these ventures" [65] (**strong**). Rogers, one of the approach's leading HCI practitioners, in a passage verified character-for-character from the source PDF: "A problem with the distributed cognition approach is that it is not a methodology that one can readily pick off the shelf and apply to a design problem. Moreover it is no panacea for CSCW or HCI… a tremendous amount of time can be spent analysing just a 2 second clip of video. One might reasonably ask is it worth it?" [66] (**strong**).

Thirteen years after the founding paper, Kirsh's TOCHI article promising "new principles for better designs" concluded with open questions and the concession that the community had accepted the core view "without adequate empirical and theoretical support" [67] (**strong**). The most serious operationalisation, DiCoT, yields an 18-item review checklist and describes itself as "one possible approach… rather than a complete account"; its authors' own assessment of a full control-room analysis was that "those design ideas that appear to have the best chance of improving the current system were not found to be big structural changes, but incremental modifications" [68] (**moderate**). Twenty years of downstream DiCoT applications are descriptive throughout, with the most recent noting that "a practical evaluation method that examines how this approach supports cognition and teamwork is missing" [69]. **In 26 years there is no controlled study showing that a distributed-cognition-informed design outperforms a comparable design produced without it** — and a targeted search for one found only further case studies.

The extended mind thesis is worse for engineering purposes, because Clark & Chalmers explicitly decline to make any prediction: asked whether the internet counts, they answer "We do not think that there are categorical answers to all of these questions, and we will not give them", and the only downstream consequences they name are philosophical, methodological and moral [70] (**strong**). Their most-quoted case is a supposition, not a result ("We can also suppose, not unrealistically, that some speed advantage accrues…").

What *is* usable from this family sits in adjacent programmes that do not depend on the DCog framework:

- **Representational determinism** — informationally equivalent representations are not computationally equivalent, producing large reliable differences in solution time, steps and errors depending on how constraints are split between internal and external form [71], anchored better by Larkin & Simon's canonical statement and corroborated across independent domains [72] (**moderate** as a specific study — single lab, no independent replication of the isomorph experiments — **strong** as a phenomenon). Design import: a constraint that lives only in documentation or a validation error is a measurably harder representation than the same constraint embodied in what the user manipulates.
- **Clark & Chalmers' four "glue and trust" criteria** as a *heuristic*, not evidence: constancy of consultation, frictionless availability, automatic endorsement, and — the one with engineering teeth — "the information in the notebook has been consciously endorsed at some point in the past, and indeed is there as a consequence of this endorsement" [70] (**weak**; a thought experiment). Auto-ingested, model-generated and user-endorsed content are categorically different and should be distinguishable at retrieval time.
- **Halverson's four-attribute test** for whether any theory earns space in a design document — descriptive, rhetorical, inferential, application power — with her verdict that DCog supplies considerable *descriptive* power and low rhetorical power because "DCog names almost none of its theoretical constructs" [73] (**moderate**). Her assessment is more favourable than commonly reported, and the common dismissal that DCog "treats people and artifacts as interchangeable" she rebuts directly: the symmetry is analytic, not ontological.
- **Höök & Löwgren's intermediate-level knowledge**: HCI "mainly produces knowledge on the levels of instances and theories", and the generative territory is between them — strong concepts, patterns, annotated portfolios — with theory annotating design examples rather than replacing them [74] (**moderate**). This determines the *form* of a design document: organise it around a few named generative concepts tied to worked examples, and let theory appear as annotation.

One transferable mechanism does come from the ethnography: in Hutchins' cockpit, the same speed is carried simultaneously in permanent paper, physical state, ephemeral speech and internal memory, and the work involves "cross modality consistency checking" [75] (**weak**; single site, no error-rate measurement, no comparison condition). Redundant representation across different media is what makes cross-checking possible — a testable hypothesis for conflict detection, not a warranted principle.

### What the evidence does not support

Each item below is self-contained and quotable.

**"84% of knowledge management programmes fail."** The primary source says the opposite. Lucier & Torsilieri (1997), retrieved and read in full: "about one-sixth of these programs achieve very significant impact within the first two years; half achieve small but important benefits; and the remaining third — the failures — have little business impact." Their own failure figure is **one third**. The 84% is 1 minus one-sixth, produced by relabelling the half that achieved "small but important benefits" as failures. Nor is it a measurement: it is prefaced "We estimate", based on "our five years of involvement… and in discussions with participants in more than 70 leading programs", with no sampling frame and no impact criterion [1].

**"70% of KM / change / digital initiatives fail."** Hughes reviewed the five most prominent published sources of this figure and found in every case an absence of valid and reliable empirical evidence — each states the number without support, or cites another source that does [2].

**A KM failure base rate is obtainable with a better survey.** It is the general pathology of IT-failure statistics. Eveleens & Verhoef applied the Standish CHAOS definitions to 5,457 forecasts of 1,211 real projects and found them "misleading, one-sided, pervert the estimation practice, and result in meaningless figures", with success rates of 67%, 5.8% and 59% across three organisations driven purely by estimation bias [4]. The KM literature is additionally structurally incapable of producing one, since the multi-case studies code from other people's published cases and concede that "organizations do not provide accurate reports of failure in their projects" [5].

**"Knowledge workers spend 2.5 hours a day searching for information" (IDC).** *Provenance failure.* The white paper lists this under a heading titled *Assumptions*: "We use a general estimate that the typical knowledge worker spends about 2.5 hours per day, or roughly 30% of the workday, searching for information… IDC believes the number represents a general average." No survey, sample or instrumentation is reported. The document is dated July 2001, numbered 01C2996TELECO2996, and marked "Sponsored by Inktomi" — a search vendor. Its own Scenario 1 yields $2.5M per 1,000 knowledge workers, not the $5M usually quoted [3].

**"80% (or 90%) of organisational knowledge is tacit / locked in employees' heads."** *Provenance failure, and the most load-bearing one in this category.* Three citation chains, two re-verified verbatim against the source texts, all terminate in non-research artefacts: the 90% variant runs through Smith (2001) — who hedges with "Reportedly" — to three trade magazines [41]; the 42% variant runs through Serban & Luan (2002) to a dead British Telecom marketing microsite [42]; the 80% "Delphi Group" variant appears only in formulaic secondary phrasing with no report title, year, sample or method. The defensible statement is that **no primary source is locatable despite targeted searching**. Worse than unsourced: under Polanyi's actual formulation the quantity is undefined, because tacit knowing is an aspect of all knowing rather than a disjoint set of items — "all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge. A wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable" [43] — so there is no partition to take a percentage of, whatever X is.

**"Polanyi distinguished two types of knowledge, tacit and explicit."** He did not, and this is the most frequent misreading in Grant's citation audit: "the suggestion that Polanyi identifies two types of knowledge — tacit and explicit — and that this is an either/or state. This is really in direct contradiction to his view that all knowledge has a tacit element and that the degree of tacitness varies" [44]. Note also that the sentence usually quoted to support the correction is from Polanyi's 1966 *Philosophy* article "The Logic of Tacit Inference", **not** *The Tacit Dimension* [43] — citing it to the latter is itself an instance of the sloppiness being documented.

**"Tacit knowledge can be converted to explicit through a managed process (SECI), so a capture system will extract it."** Contested, not established — and equally, **not refuted**. SECI's own validating survey measured semantic information creation rather than knowledge, addressed content while SECI is a process model, and reported variance explained of 51% (externalization) and 56% (internalization) against a conventional 60–70% threshold; the audit's conclusion is "no unambiguous evidence at all for any of the four modes", with a conceded "tentative support for externalization" [46]. But independent confirmatory studies exist (Dyck et al. 2005; Akbar & Tzokas 2013), unassessed by this review, and Nonaka & von Krogh reassert that conversion is explained "theoretically and empirically" [47]. Telling a technical reviewer that SECI has been empirically refuted is an overclaim that will not survive.

**"The Matsushita bread machine shows tacit knowledge successfully externalised."** Two independent peer-reviewed re-analyses reach convergent negative verdicts by different routes: kneading does not affect the taste of bread, so the team "solved the taste problem accidentally" and "no 'knowledge' about making tasty bread whatsoever was transferred" [46]; and an action-morphicity analysis showing the machine mimics only special kinds of human action and functions as a "social prosthesis" [76]. All are conceptual re-analyses of the same published secondary account, none has primary access to the events, and Nonaka & von Krogh contest them [47].

**"The knowledge-management literature is a mature evidence base you can mine for design guidance."** Grant's audit of 52 papers citing Polanyi found 37% clearly showed evidence of having read him, 42% unlikely to have, and 23% misinterpreting — affecting over 40% of the papers that actually engage with tacit knowledge [44] (single-coder inspection; direction credible, figures indicative). Two-thirds of the field sits in the Conversion perspective its own critics call incoherent [45].

**"If you make contributing cheap and frictionless enough, people will contribute."** Cost reduction is necessary and demonstrably insufficient, and there is now an experiment as well as an argument. One third of participants refused to contribute to a discretionary database "even when the cost was minimal" [9]; and Narayan et al.'s large-scale invitation-based field experiment on a gamified tutorial designed to lower exactly this barrier found "no effect of either using the tutorial or of being invited to do so over a period of 180 days" [11].

**"People withhold knowledge to protect their status, power or image."** Failed its most direct quantitative test. Kankanhalli, Tan & Wei modelled loss of knowledge power and loss of image explicitly as costs in a social-exchange model of repository contribution and report: "The loss of knowledge power and image do not appear to impact EKR usage by knowledge contributors" [13]. The vivid contrary field quotes come from a five-month early-rollout study in an up-or-out consultancy [8] — arguably the maximum-status-anxiety setting, which is the point: the mechanism appears contingent on the reward structure rather than being a general property of experts.

**"Making contribution visible suppresses it, so hide contribution metrics by default."** Contested, and the large-N evidence points the other way: image loss and knowledge-power loss did not predict withholding [13], and Wasko & Faraj found people contribute *because* it "enhances their professional reputations" [14]. The supportable narrower rule concerns **routing** — keep an escalation path that surfaces problems to management separate from the capture path — not hiding peer-visible attribution, which is one of the few contribution drivers with positive evidence behind it.

**"Rewarding contribution increases knowledge sharing."** Contested. Bock et al.: "Contrary to common belief, we find anticipated extrinsic rewards exert a negative effect on individuals' knowledge-sharing attitudes" [12]. Kankanhalli et al. find extrinsic benefits contingent on trust, norms and identification while intrinsic benefits are unmoderated [13]. A 46-study meta-analysis reports a positive aggregate with "high between-study variability" and names its own "cooperation bias" — participants likely overestimate their willingness to share [77]. Every study in this family measures intention or self-reported usage, never observed contribution.

**"Quality control and validation protect the knowledge base and therefore improve it."** Contradicted at scale, though the causal claim is observational. Halfaker et al. show Wikipedia's quality-control apparatus "implicated as key causes of decreased newcomer retention", with good-faith newcomer proportion constant while rejection rose; editors reverting with automated tools engaged in follow-up discussion ~7% of the time versus ~60% for manual reverters [78]. The paired experiments sharpen it: the gamified tutorial produced nothing [11], while a *staffed human help space* improved retention "for both low- and high-activity newcomers, and for both short- and long-term survival" [16].

**"Showing contributors their contributions were used will increase contribution."** Half-supported, and the other half comes from the same page of the same source. Cabrera & Cabrera do recommend use-feedback, then immediately warn that "negative feedback might reduce an employee's information self-efficacy and, consequently, reduce the likelihood that the employee will choose to contribute to the database in the future" [9]. Nothing tests either direction.

**"Formal design-rationale capture (IBIS and successors) simply failed."** The source sentence, in full, reverses the sense: "The methods resulted in **long-term cost reductions**, but success relied on severe social pressure, extensive training, or continuing human facilitation" [20]. What is documented is that DR capture requires a funded facilitation apparatus to sustain, and that outsiders could not be persuaded to adopt it — the same failure shape as a departed wiki gardener, not a refutation of the representation.

**"A well-designed controlled vocabulary or taxonomy will solve findability."** Measured to fail on two independent lines. Furnas et al.: "In every case two people favored the same term with probability <0.20", implying "80–90 percent failure rates" for single-designer-word access, with the derived optimum being "unlimited aliasing" [57]. And Hawking & Zobel, in the most favourable published institutional setting: 43.5% of pages carried no subject metadata, 50.7% of those that did repeated it verbatim in 20+ other pages, metadata was "of no use" in 37 of 85 closely examined queries, and adding subject and description metadata to a title+content baseline **degraded** performance [55].

**"Enterprise corpora lack link-based authority, which is why enterprise search underperformed."** Two errors. Enterprise webs are richly linked, and anchor text is the strongest query-dependent signal available [51][52]. What fails is *indegree-style authority* — and PageRank fails to beat indegree on comparably-sized web collections too [53]. The real absences are author-side optimisation ("deposited, not published") and link structure in non-web stores. Symmetrically, the over-correction "PageRank is worthless in the enterprise" is also wrong: IBM's own tables put it third-most-influential at ranks 3–5, decaying to negligible by rank 20 [51].

**"Pulling in external/global web signals will improve retrieval over an organisation's own corpus."** Tested and not supported: no difference between a local appliance and site-restricted global Google over 1,266 navigational queries; across seven organisations, 31 queries improved and 43 worsened out of 1,694, because external authors link overwhelmingly to home pages and "seldom reference the complex, parameterised URLs in common use in many organisations" [54]. Hold it loosely — the crawls "were not run to completion" and answer presence was largely unverified.

**"User satisfaction, complaint volume or thumbs-up rates tell you whether retrieval is working."** The oldest result in the area says the opposite: attorneys believed they had retrieved about 75% of the relevant documents when roughly 20% had been retrieved, attributed to the inherent ambiguity of language [59][60]. Users do not know what they missed, so satisfaction measures *noticed* failure only. This directly qualifies the 62%-human-factors finding [61], which analyses self-selected dissatisfaction reports: it describes what people complain about and is silent about the failures nobody noticed.

**"A benchmark score tells you which system is better."** Only under two conditions violated by default. Change the judges and system ordering changes — bronze judges cannot substitute for topic and task experts [62], and primary-versus-secondary assessors gave rankings at Kendall's τ = 0.763 over 48 topics [63]. Change how you sample queries and estimates move sharply; a sitemap-derived set reordered the engines outright [64]. A benchmark number without a stated judge class, query-sampling method and judging protocol is not interpretable.

**"You cannot externalise a transactive memory system — it needs shared experience to form."** † Contradicted. Groups given written information about members' expertise performed comparably to groups that trained together, and a review of 64 studies concludes direct interpersonal communication is not necessary for TMS development or utilization [29]. Experimental TMS structure is routinely created by handing participants written (category → person) rows [79]. Wegner's own chapter treats books, files and computers as the same architecture as people, with people as one class of "location" [27]. *Caveat: the load-bearing primary (Moreland & Myaskovsky [30]) was not obtainable by the source review, and that review received no adversarial pass.*

**"Building a who-knows-what index is the high-leverage move — pointers beat content."** † Half-supported, and the popular half is the wrong half. Compilational (who-knows-what) representations do beat compositional (shared-knowledge) ones meta-analytically (ρ .44 vs .32 overall; .42 vs .26 objective), but the TMS *specialization* subscale alone — the bare pointer-differentiation measure — predicts at ρ .35 / .29, indistinguishable in magnitude from shared-knowledge measures. Global TMS reaches .47 only when *credibility* and *coordination* are included [28]. A pointer index with no reliability signal and no retrieval machinery should be expected to perform like a plain topic index.

**"An external store degrades or atrophies the interpersonal transactive memory."** † No good evidence, and some against: repository use and person-to-person exchange interact rather than trade off [31]; digital repository use *weakened* the negative effect of remoteness on accuracy of expert recognition; and offloading to a store believed reliable improved subsequent learning [39]. Every result is cross-sectional or single-session; no longitudinal study of substitution exists. What *is* supported is a narrower and different claim: using stored documents can hurt the outcome for experienced teams and teams needing to differentiate [36] — task fit, not memory atrophy.

**"The 'Google effect' shows the internet has become our transactive memory."** The load-bearing experiment does not replicate. Sparrow et al.'s Experiment 1 (the "Google Stroop" priming effect) failed the Social Sciences Replication Project (p = .26 first round, .45 pooled) [80] and failed an independent direct replication; when the original author objected to the replication's design, Hesselmann re-ran it "with a research design closer to the original experiment" and still found "no conclusive evidence" [81]. **Important scope correction:** only Experiment 1 was replicated. Experiments 2–4 — including the location-over-content result (N=32) that is actually the claim used in product writing — have **no direct replication in either direction** [82]. Do not cite "the Google effect failed to replicate" as covering the retention claim, and do not assume the retention claim is safe.

**"Offloading to an external store reliably improves memory for what remains."** Weakened. The first meta-analysis of the offloading literature finds "the benefit of offloading is greater for forced- compared to choice-offloading conditions" — and product use is a choice condition — and that offloading "reduces interindividual variability" rather than lifting the mean [38]. Preregistered follow-up work finds the saving-enhanced memory effect asymmetric and order-dependent, explicitly identifying "boundary conditions" [40]. The reliability precondition rests on a single manipulation in a single experiment that has never been independently replicated [39].

**"More sharing and more retrieval means better performance, so maximise coverage and retrieval volume."** † Contradicted on both halves. In a 139-team, 743-person field study knowledge *sharing* had no direct effect on team performance; the entire effect ran through knowledge *application* [35]. And in 182 consulting sales proposals with an objective win/loss outcome, highly experienced teams were **more** likely to lose if they obtained and used electronic documents or colleague advice [36].

**"Cumulative volume — documents indexed, entries captured — is a reasonable proxy for how much the organisation knows."** † Explicitly refuted in the foundational organisational-learning study: "knowledge acquired through production depreciates rapidly. The conventional measure of learning, cumulative output, significantly overstates the persistence of learning" [23]. Cumulative index size is the exact analogue.

**"Externalising knowledge into a shared store reliably improves team performance, and teams will tell you it does."** The measurement instrument roughly doubles the apparent effect: across 44 laboratory studies and 103 effect sizes the TMS–outcome relationship is r = .44, but self-report scales yield r = .77 versus observer ratings .38 and embedded behavioural metrics .39 — the two behavioural instruments agree with each other and disagree with self-report, identifying self-report as the biased one [33]. Contested: an earlier meta-analysis found operationalisation did *not* moderate [34].

**"In a diverse expert team, people naturally work out who knows what — you do not need to build it."** † Backwards. A meta-analysis of 76 studies (6,869 sampling units) finds informational and gender diversity **negatively** associated with TMS development [37]. The teams that most need a map are the least likely to form one spontaneously.

**"Clark and Chalmers argued the internet is part of your mind."** The primary text says the opposite: "The Internet is likely to fail on multiple counts, unless I am unusually computer-reliant, facile with the technology, and trusting, but information in certain files on my computer may qualify." The thesis requires constancy of consultation, frictionless availability and automatic endorsement — a shared, searched, sometimes-wrong corpus fails all three — and they explicitly decline the general case: "We do not think that there are categorical answers to all of these questions, and we will not give them" [70]. Anyone citing extended mind to justify a cloud knowledge base is citing against the source.

**"Distributed cognition gives you a method for deriving system requirements from field observation."** Its own leading practitioners deny it in print: "it is not a methodology that one can readily pick off the shelf and apply to a design problem… One might reasonably ask is it worth it?" [66]. The founding paper concedes "the integrated research program described in this paper does not yet exist" [65]; the most serious operationalisation calls itself "one possible approach… rather than a complete account" [68]; and twenty years of DiCoT applications remain descriptive, with practitioners noting that "a practical evaluation method… is missing" [69]. **In 26 years no controlled study shows that a distributed-cognition-informed design outperforms a comparable design produced without it.**

**"Epistemic action is a well-quantified effect — acting on the world is an order of magnitude faster than computing in the head."** The famous 100 ms versus 800–1200 ms comparison rests on a self-described **pilot study with three participants**, and the authors present the mental-rotation half as an estimate ("we estimate that it takes in the neighborhood of 800 to 1200 ms") [83]. The epistemic/pragmatic distinction is sound; the multiplier is not a benchmark.

**"The social-technical gap is a temporary limitation that better technology — now, LLMs — will close."** Ackerman anticipated and rejected this in 2000, naming it as one of two remaining counter-arguments, and the specific instances he named remain unchanged twenty-five years later [24]. State the honest limit: this is an argument from track record, not a proof, and Ackerman says so himself. What the literature does establish is narrower and still binding — the documented failure modes are representational and social (nuance, ambiguity, incentive disparity, decontextualisation, unstaffed maintenance), so a technique that only lowers articulation *cost* does not by itself address them; Kankanhalli et al. make it concrete, since codification effort's effect is moderated by norms and removing the effort does not remove the moderator [13].

**"There is no evidence about whether AI can capture tacit knowledge."** Correct, and it cuts both ways — which is the part usually omitted. The systematic review states that "none of the reviewed studies has focused on exploring the development of AI in relation to TK in the workplace" [45]; its own deflationary argument is a Practice-perspective assertion, not a demonstration, and predates instruction-tuned models, while the Interactional camp in the same review accepts that converted bodily tacit knowledge can be used by machines. Do not cite this literature as showing that AI cannot; it shows that nobody looked. The same holds for Markus's intermediary role: her abstract already names "human and **technical** intermediaries", so automating it is a direction the source contemplates rather than endorses — and no study tests whether automated repackaging achieves what human knowledge managers achieved [19].

**A closing note on repeating any of the above outward.** Letrud & Hernes examined 613 articles citing three myth-exposing publications and found 468 affirmed the myth anyway, concluding that "if the intention behind the critiques were to reduce the frequency of affirmations of the claim in the scientific corpus, they may have achieved the very opposite" [84] (**moderate**; three probes at *one* myth, in academic citation behaviour, not marketing copy — the transfer is an inference, not a finding). Weak support for keeping the provenance analysis internal and simply making a different, defensible claim externally.

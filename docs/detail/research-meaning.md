# Research detail: meaning, vocabulary and identity

Supporting detail for [../research.md](../research.md). Claims are graded strong / moderate / weak / contested.

## Meaning, vocabulary and identity

Three questions decide how much semantic machinery `kc` should carry: whether a controlled vocabulary earns its keep, whether a knowledge graph earns its keep, and what happens when the thing that decides "these two are the same" is wrong in a store that never forgets. The literature answers the first two more clearly than its reputation suggests — mostly in the negative, and mostly with a narrow exception that is worth building. It answers the third with a specific, asymmetric hazard that an append-only design makes permanent.

**Evidence-provenance note.** The ontology/controlled-vocabulary and enterprise-knowledge-graph source reviews both received an adversarial verification pass, with quantitative claims re-checked against primary sources and several prior gradings downgraded; findings drawn from them are stated at the strength that pass left them. The entity-resolution review did **not** receive that pass. Every ER figure below is therefore marked *(unverified pass)* on first use and should be treated as reported-not-confirmed: directionally usable for design, not quotable as fact without re-checking the primary source.

### What SKOS and ISO 25964 actually specify

SKOS normatively specifies a data model and an interchange vocabulary, and deliberately specifies **no retrieval behaviour at all** [1]. `skos:broader` is explicitly not transitive and is a sub-property of the transitive `skos:broaderTransitive` (S22), so transitive closure is opt-in; `skos:related` is symmetric (S23) and disjoint from `skos:broaderTransitive` (S27); `skos:exactMatch` is symmetric and transitive (S44/S45) and disjoint from broad- and related-match (S46) [1]. The specification's own editors state that how a retrieval application uses broader/narrower/related to improve recall "is not defined by the SKOS specifications and is left to the application" [2]. *(strong — standard read directly, plus the editors' peer-reviewed design account.)*

The integrity conditions people most want from SKOS are not machine-enforced. S13 (prefLabel/altLabel/hiddenLabel pairwise disjoint), S14 (at most one prefLabel per language tag), S27 and S46 are stated as integrity *conditions*, not OWL axioms, and the Reference says how an application handles a violating statement "is not defined in this specification and may vary between applications" [1]. The spec also declines to assert either reflexivity or irreflexivity of `skos:broader`, so reflexive broader and `broaderTransitive` cycles are consistent with the model **by design** [1]. Practical consequence: "valid SKOS" guarantees neither a DAG nor one preferred label per language, and any such invariant is a validator you write and run as a build step. *(strong.)*

| Capability | SKOS | ISO 25964 | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concept identity, labels, notes, mapping properties | yes | yes | SKOS's five mapping properties are strictly coarser than ISO's equivalence trichotomy [2][3] |
| Hierarchy subtypes (generic BTG / instantial BTI / partitive BTP) | **no** — deliberately postponed by the W3C WG to avoid overlap with `rdfs:subClassOf` and `rdf:type` | yes | filled by the `iso-thes` / SKOS-Thes namespace [2][4] |
| Term-level model, compound equivalence, split non-preferred terms | **no** | yes | `iso-thes` adds `CompoundEquivalence`, `PreferredTerm`, `SplitNonPreferredTerm` as skos-xl subclasses [4] |
| Thesaurus arrays, node labels, concept groups, microthesauri, term status | **no** | yes | `ThesaurusArray`, `ConceptGroup`, `microThesaurusOf`, `status` [4] |
| Exact / inexact / partial equivalence for cross-vocabulary mapping | **no** | yes (Part 2) | best off-the-shelf way to say "these two vocabularies partly agree" [5][3] |
| Process, construction and software requirements | **no** | yes (Part 1) | facet analysis, maintenance management, functional requirements for thesaurus software [6][7] |
| Query-time semantics | **none** | none | neither standard specifies retrieval behaviour [1][2] |
| Free normative text | yes | **no** — both parts must be purchased | only the data model, XML schema and SKOS correspondence are free from NISO [3] |

The ISO row deserves a caveat the source review flagged explicitly: the normative text is paywalled and the ISO catalogue pages refuse automated retrieval, so the clause-level Part 2 description above rests on free NISO adjunct materials, the `iso-thes` namespace and the standards editors' own published account [3][4][7] — not on the standard. *(moderate.)* The practical form of this is: you can build an ISO-25964-**compatible** model for free; you cannot claim conformance, and you do not get the process guidance without paying.

Adoption cost is therefore three things, none of them the licence fee: the validator you must write because SKOS does not enforce its own integrity conditions; the query-time semantics you must write and measure because neither standard supplies any; and the defensive ingestion you must write because deployed vocabularies violate the guidelines. Every one of fifteen major SKOS vocabularies audited with qSKOS deviates from published guidelines — LCSH has 342,848 of 443,164 concepts undocumented, 173,149 orphans and 22,343 weakly connected components; DBpedia Categories has 1,132 hierarchy cycles; AGROVOC is entirely undocumented [8]. That audit is graded **moderate, not strong**: its own language is "possible quality issues" against its authors' rubric on a 2012 snapshot, and it measured no downstream harm whatsoever. Use it to justify cycle-breaking, orphan buckets and label-language fallbacks on ingest; do not use it to argue published vocabularies are unusable.

### Controlled vocabulary versus free tagging: what retrieval experiments actually show

| Task shape | Result | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Ranked ad-hoc retrieval | Controlled-vocabulary-only representations were **32–96% worse in MAP** than a plain-text baseline across TREC Genomics 2004–2007; combining text+MeSH gave significant gains for exactly one of six concept assigners (KNN: +12/+15/+11/+10% MAP). Authors' conclusion, verbatim: "a mixed textual and conceptual representation only improves retrieval if the classification is of high quality" [9]. Consistent with Cranfield's finding that single-word terms beat concept terms and controlled vocabulary [10] | **strong** (one domain, one collection family, 2009-era classifiers) |
| Automatic thesaurus/ontology query expansion | **Split along the task axis.** Negative on ranked retrieval: Voorhees got improvement only on short queries [11], and her *earlier* SIGIR '93 WordNet sense-disambiguation work degraded retrieval [12]; Smeaton & Berrut and Richardson & Smeaton likewise failed [13]. Positive on Boolean retrieval: Lu, Kim & Wilbur, on the same ~160k-citation collection, found MeSH expansion "can generally improve retrieval performance" — while adding that "the improvement may not affect end PubMed users in realistic situations" [14] | **contested** |
| Exhaustive-recall / faceted navigation | Faceted controlled metadata beat a keyword baseline on full recall: 81% vs 57% and 77% vs 21%, with 90–91% preference [15]. **But the comparison is confounded**: the faceted interface was an order of magnitude slower per step (3.7s vs 0.3s), participants were *given three extra minutes* in that condition on the recall task, and spent significantly longer on task. On the simple single-facet task, preference split 16–15 — a tie. n=32, one collection, never replicated | **moderate**, downgraded from strong |
| Marginal recall of an extra descriptive field | 35.9% mean (30.2% median, 35.4% aggregate) of catalogue keyword hits matched **only** in a subject-heading field; for 31.7% of searches, half or more of hits would be lost [16]. A 2015 replication after table-of-contents and summary enrichment still lost roughly a quarter to a third [17]. In a systematic-review setting an all-MeSH strategy retrieved 75% of a 1,521-article gold standard against 54% for a text-word strategy (precision 47.7% vs 34.4%) [18] — but see below for why that comparison is badly biased | **moderate** — measures *more text*, not *controlled-ness* |
| Tags vs controlled vocabulary, head to head | On >2M book records and >330 real search requests (LCSH on 1.25M, DDC on 1.15M), **tags performed better overall**, controlled terms were complementary and improved search, and neither was wholly suited to complex needs [19] | **moderate** — argues for hybrid, against replacement |

The underlying constraint is real and severe: two people spontaneously choose the same term for an object with probability **below 0.20** in every one of five domains, so a single designer-chosen term produces 80–90% failure and the derived optimum is "unlimited aliasing" [20]. *(strong.)* The design reading is that the payoff of a vocabulary lives in the **aliases** (`altLabel`/`hiddenLabel`/synonym rings), not the hierarchy — and that a 1987 pre-embedding measurement of spontaneous *naming* does not establish that a curated alias set still adds anything on top of a modern dense retriever.

Free tagging does not reliably self-organise either, and the fix is cheap. Halpin et al.'s power-law stabilisation holds only for **heavily tagged** resources — the paper's own caveat is that the analysis "refers to heavily tagged sites (tagged more than 1000 times), and considers the most used [25 tags]" [21]. On Stack Overflow, tag efficiency was highest at a limit of **one tag per item** and degraded nonlinearly as the limit rose; questions with composite tags showed higher content, retrieval efficiency and descriptiveness, and 70 of 100 manually inspected composite tags encoded a hierarchy [22]. That system simultaneously caps tags, encourages composite names and restricts new-tag creation to experienced users, so its emergence is a consequence of constraints — and the authors explicitly disclaim causality for the cap. *(contested.)* This is nonetheless the cheapest and most reversible intervention in the whole literature: cap tags per item at a low single digit, permit composite/hyphenated tag names so users encode hierarchy you never build, gate creation of *new* tags while letting anyone apply existing ones — and A/B test it, because nobody has.

The gating precondition for all of this is tagger quality, and the best available natural experiment on that is fresh and negative. NLM fully automated MEDLINE MeSH indexing in mid-2022 after two decades of refining the Medical Text Indexer on its own corpus; an independent audit of records indexed by MTI-Auto in early 2023 found only **53%** had indexing representing the article's main concepts, with 47% carrying inadequacies capable of affecting retrieval — inappropriate MeSH from acronyms and evocative language, over-general terms where a precise one existed, and significant concepts omitted entirely [23]. A separate pilot found systematic check-tag ranking errors and omissions against human indexers [24]. *(moderate — two appropriateness audits, neither a retrieval experiment.)* Combined with MeSH Up's precondition [9], the rule falls out cleanly: **build the tagger's evaluation set before you build the vocabulary**, and gate the vocabulary behind a measured quality bar.

Do not, however, evaluate a tagger by agreement with one human. Markey's review of 25 interindexer-consistency studies reported consistency from 82% down to 4% [25] — but that finding is **weak**: the underlying studies predate 1980, and her own frequently quoted 7% figure came from three non-specialist indexers on 100 works of art *with no controlled vocabulary at all*, which is a worst case, not a human ceiling.

### Vocabulary maintenance and staleness

Formal vocabularies change fast enough to change conclusions drawn from them. Gene Ontology went from 16,139 to 40,810 terms, 21,998 to 78,078 inter-term relationships and 19,616 to 109,162 annotations between 2004 and 2015; across 104 multi-cohort analyses spanning 92 diseases, 377 datasets and >23,000 samples, enrichment computed with the 2004 ontology matched the 2015 result with a **median Jaccard consistency of 0.27**, and worse (0.038–0.1) when annotations were varied [26]. Coverage is permanently lopsided: 58% of annotations sit on 16% of human genes. GO releases daily; NCI Thesaurus monthly [27]. *(strong.)* Two design consequences follow directly: any artifact derived from a vocabulary must record the vocabulary **version** or it is not reproducible, and "no concept assigned" must be modelled as *unknown*, never as *not about that topic*.

The expensive part of a vocabulary is not building it — it is knowing what breaks when it changes. The canonical evolution survey decomposes the problem into five tasks (detect need, suggest changes, validate changes, **assess impact on dependent artefacts**, manage changes) and reports that formal change validation is well covered while impact assessment is covered by a handful of works, with only two surveyed frameworks having any equivalent step; it also notes that manual change recording is often incomplete or erroneous even with curators' best efforts, and that version-compatibility determination cannot be fully automated [28]. *(moderate.)* Build the impact-assessment step first — *what content is annotated with the concept I am about to merge or deprecate?* — and record changes automatically from the editing surface rather than asking curators to log them.

The economic argument for building a formal vocabulary is reuse, and reuse largely does not happen in the form the argument assumes. Across **337** BioPortal ontologies (not 377 — the prior review's figure was wrong), term overlap was 25.3–30.2% but measured reuse was **<9%**, with most ontologies reusing under 5% of terms and reused terms >90% semantically similar within a cluster, i.e. shallow sibling and parent-child borrowing [29]. The authors' own qualifier, which is load-bearing: "we found strong indications that users actually intended to reuse terms, but in many cases they used incorrect representations" — so the <9% partly measures representational heterogeneity (IRI vs xref vs CUI), not unwillingness. Practitioners report "soft reuse" (copying concepts rather than importing) at 74.3% among the highly experienced [30], and LOV-registry studies report substantially higher reuse, so the magnitude is repository-dependent. *(moderate, downgraded.)* The design seam this argues for is **"resolve an external concept URI to a set of strings"**, not "import a graph and reason over it".

And the published ontology web is in poor repair and vanishes. Of 735 crawled ontologies, only 275 had any detectable licence and 137 a machine-readable one; 702 had documentation-test violations; 464 (63%) scored under two of four consumer-oriented stars [31]; a follow-up frames unavailable ontologies as a direct reproducibility problem for LOD-based work [32]. *(moderate — a single 2020 crawl against the authors' own rubric.)* Never dereference a vocabulary URI at runtime: vendor a versioned snapshot the way you pin a dependency, and assume you cannot legally redistribute it.

Finally, there is **no usable a-priori cost model**. ONTOCOM, the only serious attempt, is COCOMO-style, calibrated on 36 data points, with roughly 75% of points inside ±75% of the estimate and materially worse coverage at ±30%; its authors report that ontology *evaluation*, domain analysis and conceptualization dominate effort, and no comparable model exists for **maintenance** at all [33]. *(weak.)* Do not put a cost estimate for vocabulary work in a design doc — timebox instead: build the smallest vocabulary that answers a named competency question, ship it, measure it.

### Enterprise knowledge graphs: what construction and maintenance actually cost

The industrial record is consistent about quality and silent about headcount.

| System | Scale | Measured quality | Strength |
|---|---|---|---|
| Google Knowledge Vault [34] | ~1.6B candidate triples | Fused extractor yielded 100M at confidence >0.9 — a **6%** trust rate — at AUC 0.927. The headline 271M "confident facts" is reached only by fusing extractions with Freebase-derived graph priors, and "about 1/3 of the 271M confident triples were not previously in Freebase" | **strong** (2014-era extraction) |
| Amazon AutoKnow [35] | >1B triples, 11K product types | Type triples 87.7% precision, hypernyms 88.1%, value synonyms 91.6/93.7% — but for attribute values, "the defect rate is at best 21.1%, and can be over 90% in certain cases", with per-(domain, attribute) defect rates 21.14–98.58% | **strong** |
| Apple Saga [36] | production platform | Extended triples with per-fact source arrays, per-source trust scores, fused probability as metadata; non-destructive integration; per-source Added/Updated/Deleted deltas with volatile predicates factored out; per-consumer materialised views each carrying a freshness SLA | **moderate** — paywalled; metadata verified, internal quotations carried over unverified |
| Bosch ontology reshaping [37] | one welding dataset | An ontology reflecting the general domain rather than the data produced sparse, blank-node-heavy KGs needing "long and cumbersome SPARQL queries"; reshaping to the data made generation 7–8× faster, cut entities to 1/2–1/6, storage to 2/3, blank nodes to zero | **weak** — 5-page poster, self-reported, no query-correctness baseline |

Two numbers are worth carrying into a build decision. First, **graph indexing costs roughly 40–60× chunk-and-embed**: on MultiHop-RAG with the same corpus and hardware, plain RAG construction took 135s against 7,702s (triple KG) and 5,560s (community) [38]; an independent accounting put Microsoft GraphRAG's Tech-QA indexing at 49.3M prompt + 35.7M completion + 54.0M embedding tokens against VectorRAG's zero LLM tokens and 3.0M embedding tokens [39]. That multiplier recurs on every content change, which is what makes graphs expensive for a *living* corpus rather than a static one. Query latency is a separate matter — community-based retrieval is actually *faster* at query time than plain RAG (1,249s vs 1,724s); only the triple-based variant carries the ~8× query penalty. *(moderate — two preprints with reproducible cost accounting.)*

Second, **enterprise ontologies are lightweight in practice**. Summarising industrial accounts, Hogan et al. report that "the ontologies used tend to be lightweight, often simple taxonomies representing a hierarchy of classes or concepts" [40]. Amazon corroborates the cost split from the other side: for a bounded domain (movies: 52 entity types, 155 relationships) an ontology "can be manually defined within weeks, especially by leveraging existing data sources", while for open product retail they judged it infeasible and went fully automatic [35]. *(moderate.)* Scope by domain boundedness, not ambition: ~50–150 terms is weeks of hand work and worth doing; an open domain is a shallow taxonomy plus automation. Construction and maintenance cost **in person-months remains essentially unpublished** — every industrial paper reports throughput, cluster configuration and token counts, and none reports headcount or calendar duration; Amazon's "weeks" remark is the only credible anchor in the corpus.

One structural warning from a deployed system: single-parent taxonomies are the wrong default. AutoKnow's lessons section says "we miss multiple parents… there is often no clear cut for product types: one product can be both fashion swimwear and two-piece swimwear. In general, we need to extend concepts to model broadly subtype, synonym, and overlapping relationships" [35]. *(moderate.)* Retrofitting multiple inheritance onto a tree after content is classified is a migration, not an edit.

### Does a graph improve downstream QA over a strong retrieval baseline?

Not reliably, and the popular claim is narrower than it is repeated as. The originating GraphRAG result generated its own questions with an LLM, had **no gold answers**, and explicitly set aside "context relevance", "faithfulness" and "answer relevance" as "not relevant to global sensemaking"; it judged comprehensiveness, diversity and empowerment via LLM-as-judge, reporting win rates of 72–83% (podcast) and 72–80% (news) for comprehensiveness and 75–82% / 62–71% for diversity, all at p<.001 or p<.01, with vector RAG winning directness as a validity check [41]. *(strong — primary source verified verbatim.)* The claim is "more comprehensive and more varied answers to corpus-wide questions", not "more correct answers".

On benchmarks with gold answers the picture flattens or reverses:

| Comparison | Result | Strength |
|---|---|---|

HotpotQA: HippoRAG2 **63.01**, Community-Local 61.66, RAG 60.04, KG-triples 42.60. Summarisation is a tie (ROUGE-2 F1 ~10.1 across all). Authors' summary: "distinct strengths… across different tasks" | **moderate** — unrefereed preprint, no significance testing |
| 4-corpus independent evaluation [39] | Microsoft GraphRAG last on all four corpora and *below closed-book* on MuSiQue. **But**: HippoRAG2 (a graph method) **beats** VectorRAG on Tech-QA (0.3448 vs 0.3218) and MuSiQue (0.2196 vs 0.2007) and is within noise on the other two | **weak** — competing-method paper by an interested party (Samsung AI Warsaw) proposing its own system; three of four corpora come from RAGBench, and the authors concede VectorRAG's showing "can be partially explained by the architectural similarity of VectorRAG to the procedure for generating the original datasets" |

| Abstention (null queries) [38] | Plain RAG **96.01**, HippoRAG2 85.71, Community-Local 80.07, **Community-Global 19.27** — and 80.07 → 50.50 under one configuration change | **moderate** |

The single most transferable rule in this corpus: **the graph methods that win are the ones that keep the source text.** HippoRAG2 (graph-guided retrieval over retained chunks) beats triple-only KG-GraphRAG by 15–20 F1 on the same corpora and is best or near-best in both independent evaluations, while community-summary GraphRAG — which replaces text with LLM summaries — collapses on detail-oriented and null queries [38][39]. What the negative results disconfirm is *Microsoft-style community-summary GraphRAG*, not graph-structured retrieval. If graph structure is built at all, it should be a navigation/expansion layer over the original passages, never a replacement; lossy summarisation of source content is the specific choice that destroys abstention.

Two counter-weights keep this from collapsing into "just use vectors". First, single-vector retrieval has a **proven representational ceiling**: the number of top-k document subsets an embedding index can return is bounded by its dimension, and on the LIMIT dataset — deliberately trivial queries — SOTA embedders "struggle to reach even 20% recall@100"; on a 46-document version, recall@2 is BM25 97.8 and ColBERT 83.5 against the best single-vector model at 54.3, with several major embedders near 19, on a set representable in 12 dimensions [43]. *(strong — peer-reviewed, ICLR 2026, with a public benchmark.)* The symmetric warning is that BM25 collapses ~90% (to 10.6) on the synonym variant, so no single retriever is a safe default either. Second, **no study in this corpus benchmarks against a tuned BM25+dense hybrid with a reranker** — which is the exact failure mode IR has documented before: a meta-analysis of TREC Robust04 found no upward trend in effectiveness over a decade of claimed neural wins, because authors demonstrate gains against weak baselines [44]. *(moderate.)* Establish the cheap strong baseline — days of work — before costing a ~50× indexing multiplier.

Finally, treat every single-digit gap in this literature as noise. A NeurIPS 2025 systematic review of 445 LLM benchmarks by 29 expert reviewers found only **16.0%** used any uncertainty estimate or statistical test, fewer than 10% used complete real-world tasks, and 46.6% did not discuss construct validity [45]. *(strong.)* Applied here: the ORAN graph-vs-vector gap (0.50 vs 0.48, SDs 0.17 and 0.12) and the 8-dataset NQ gap (64.78 vs 63.01) are not results worth an indexing budget.

The one strongly positive vocabulary result in the KG literature is not about retrieval at all: using an ontology as a **deterministic validator** (Ontology-Based Query Check — rule-based validity checking plus LLM repair) raised text-to-SPARQL accuracy from 54.2% to 72.55%, with a further 8% of explicit "I don't know" responses after three failed repair iterations, for ~20% overall error [46]. *(moderate — an edited IEEE bulletin, not a refereed venue, from a knowledge-graph vendor, on a 43-question benchmark with GPT-4-era models.)* If a formal vocabulary is built, build it so a checker can run against it — SHACL-shaped [47] — not so a human can admire it.

### Entity resolution: how good it actually is

*All figures in this subsection come from a source review that did not receive an adversarial verification pass (unverified pass); treat them as reported, not confirmed.*

The canonical theory is a **three-way** decision, not a binary classifier: link / possible link (clerical review) / non-link, and the Fellegi–Sunter optimality theorem is that for fixed bounds the likelihood-ratio rule with two thresholds minimises the size of the clerical-review region [48][49]. The uncertain bucket is a first-class output of the theory, not an engineering hack. It is also the case that **the error rates the framework nominally fixes are not attained in practice** — reviewing four independent evaluations, Binette & Steorts state that the fixed error rates and estimated false-match rates "are not attained in practice" because of the conditional-independence assumption and estimation error, and note that for the 1990 US Census match thresholds were set "by 'eyeballing' lists of pairs of records brought together as candidate matches" [49][50]. A model-produced match probability is an ordering statistic, not a calibrated error rate.

On realistic data the operating point is much worse than benchmark F1 suggests. Parameterising corruptions from 11 years of the North Carolina Voter Registry (n=848,566), term-frequency-adjusted Jaro-Winkler achieved a false-match rate of **0.55%** (95% CI 0.54–0.57) at a **missed-match rate of 20.34%** [51]. Roughly one in five true co-references is missed if you want the false-merge rate under 1% on person-like entities. The error is also not evenly distributed: at a fixed missed-match rate near 0.20, Hispanic voters had 36.3% higher false-match rates and Non-Hispanic Black voters 8.6% higher than Non-Hispanic White voters, driven mainly by unequal *exposure* to identifier error rather than by a different error mechanism [51]. A single global merge threshold is a distributional decision, not a neutral one — and group-sliceable metrics are far easier to record at merge time than to retrofit onto an append-only merge log.

Two more constraints bound the whole pipeline. **Blocking sets a hard recall ceiling before any matcher runs**: a state-of-the-art self-supervised blocker grid-searched to exceed 90% pair completeness yielded pairs quality of 0.3%–16.6% on seven of eight realistic datasets [52][53], so ~10% of true co-references are structurally unreachable and the candidate set is 1:100 to 1:1000 negative. A missing link is more likely a blocking miss than a matcher error. And **identity is resolver-relative**: when NHS Digital moved from one production linkage methodology to another across 1.25 billion records, 1.1% of multi-record HES IDs were *split* and 3.7% of multi-record Person IDs were the *merger* of two or more prior IDs [54]. Upgrading the resolver should produce a reviewable diff, which it can only do if each merge records which resolver, at which version, decided it.

Published benchmark F1 of 95–99% does not describe this world. Papadakis et al. found most of 13 established benchmarks "pose rather easy classification tasks", almost linearly separable or already perfectly solved; their independent re-run of DITTO fell dramatically short of published numbers on the same data (51.46/89.43 reproduced vs 98.99 reported; 28.76/65.67 vs 100.00); and they found the benchmarks themselves suspect, noting that five "seem to involve an undocumented approach for inserting or removing an arbitrary number of negative pairs" [52]. Rebuilding the benchmarks with open entities and imbalanced labels, Wang et al. concluded the prior assumptions "conceal the main challenges of the task and therefore significantly overestimate the current progress of entity matching" [55].

### The asymmetry: a false merge is not the mirror image of a false split

By specification, a merge is unrestricted substitution. OWL 2 states that `SameIndividual(a1…an)` "allows one to use each a_i as a synonym for each a_j — that is, in any expression in the ontology containing such an axiom, a_i can be replaced with a_j without affecting the meaning of the ontology" [56]. *(strong — published standard.)* The consequence of a wrong `sameAs` is not "two nodes are joined" but "every assertion about either is now an assertion about both, transitively and forever".

That compounds at scale, and it has been measured, not merely feared. In the 558.9M-statement `owl:sameAs` network scraped from the LOD Cloud (179.7M terms, ~49M equality sets), the largest equality set contains **177,794 terms** compacted from 5,547,463 statements and demonstrably conflates Bolivia, Dublin, Coca-Cola, Albert Einstein and bare literals into one identity; in the "Barack Obama" set, exactly **two** incorrect statements caused false equivalence of 78 further terms [57]. An independent industry pipeline reached the same conclusion from scratch and named it as a lesson absent from the literature: "one false-positive link can silently merge unrelated entities — assuming 'A matches B' and 'B matches C' implies 'A matches C' lets a single bad link chain hundreds of records together, so every cross-group merge must be actively re-verified" [58].

Pairwise metrics hide exactly this error. Because ER produces *microclusters* (cluster count grows O(n)), intra-cluster pairs grow O(n) while inter-cluster pairs grow ~O(n²), so a pairwise false-positive rate **improves as the corpus grows** with no improvement in the system; and "mismatching two singleton entities has an insignificant impact on pairwise metrics compared to incorrectly joining or splitting two large clusters" [59]. Different ER metrics have been shown empirically to rank algorithms in conflicting orders [60]. Report false-merge and false-split as separate cluster-level quantities, per cluster-size bucket — never a single F1.

**The append-only risk, stated concretely.** In a store where nothing is deleted, a bad merge is not an error you fix; it is an event in the history, and the history is the product. Three things make it worse than a false split there. (1) *Asymmetric recoverability*: the default failure mode of an LLM-extracted graph is massive false **split** — Microsoft GraphRAG merges entities only when they share the same title *and* type by exact string match, and LightRAG, MS GraphRAG and HippoRAG all "rely on string-matching heuristics… leaving many duplicates unresolved" ('LLMs' / 'LLM' / 'llms' / 'Large Language Models' as separate nodes) [61][62]. A split is recoverable later by adding a link; a merge that has already propagated is not. The one place these pipelines silently false-*merge* is exact title+type collision — two different people named "John Smith", type Person — and nothing flags it. (2) *Destructive canonicalization is separately irreversible*: matching decides that records co-refer; canonicalization decides what the merged entity's attribute values **are**, and it is a distinct, under-studied pipeline stage [49][63]. Retracting a merge does not restore fused attribute values. A production team's postmortem puts it plainly: "two records merged under one identity cannot be separated once their properties have been combined, and the merge leaves no error behind" [64] — though that report is **weak** evidence, a single-team hazard narrative whose authors concede they argue rather than demonstrate and report no identity benchmark. (3) *The error is unobservable*: the same team reports that "the over-merges the key permits are undetectable by construction", and there is **no published measured false-merge rate for identity in a production LLM-constructed knowledge graph** at all.

### Standard mitigations, and which ones survive contact with append-only storage

| Mitigation | What the evidence supports | Caveat |
|---|---|---|
| **Two-tier identity vocabulary** — a transitive strong-identity predicate reserved for shared strong identifiers, plus a deliberately non-transitive "probably the same" predicate for everything a matcher produces | Standards precedent, not a bespoke invention: SKOS defines `skos:closeMatch` as symmetric but explicitly **not** transitive, "in order to avoid the possibility of 'compound errors' when combining mappings across more than two concept schemes" [1]; the "contextual identity" literature proposes the same weakening independently [65] | Cheapest available fix for chaining; untested against a query-time collapsing view |
| **Never materialise transitive closure** over pairwise identity decisions; or if you must, gate any closure that *joins two existing clusters* behind a higher evidence bar | Directly indicated by the 177,794-term blob and the 2-links-→-78-terms case [57], and independently by the industry pipeline's "every cross-group merge must be actively re-verified" [58] | Neither source proposes or evaluates a specific asymmetric threshold; the error rate for cross-cluster vs within-cluster merges has not been measured separately |
| **Merges as attributable, retractable assertions** rather than structural edits — "resolver X, version v, at time t, weight w, asserted a is-probably b, from evidence e" | The vocabulary already exists as standards: PROV-O supplies Entity/Activity/Agent with `wasDerivedFrom`/`wasGeneratedBy`/`wasAttributedTo` [66], and RDF 1.2 adds triple terms and `rdf:reifies` so a triple can be annotated with confidence or belief **without being asserted**, explicitly supporting "expressing uncertainty about whether a fact is true without asserting it as fact" [67] | RDF 1.2 is a **Candidate Recommendation**, not a Recommendation — pin the reification pattern you depend on [67]. No benchmark exists for the storage/query overhead of provenance-preserving merges at graph scale |
| **Reversible merge** — redirect the merged-away identifier, never reuse it, keep full revision history | Established production pattern: Wikidata undoes merges by restoring pre-merge revisions, recipient item first [68] | The residual irreversibility is named precisely: "if bots have already replaced redirected item IDs in statements, you must contact bot operators to reverse those automated substitutions" [68]. The undo is only as complete as the discipline of never rewriting references in place — resolve through the redirect at read time |
| **Canonicalization as a view, not a write** — store source-attributed attribute values, compute the fused entity at read time | Follows from the four-stage decomposition where canonicalization is a separate stage from matching [49][63], and from Apple's non-destructive integration with per-fact source arrays and fused probability as metadata [36] | Saga's internals are paywalled and were not independently verified |
| **A durable "unresolved / insufficient evidence" terminal state** | Four expert judges evaluating 200 real `owl:sameAs` links returned "can't tell" on **91 of 200 (45.5%)** because no sufficient description existed to determine what the terms denoted [57] | "Escalate to a human" is not a completion path when nearly half the hard cases are undecidable |
| **Clerical review spent in the middle of the score distribution** | Cutting review from ~23% to ~7% of candidate pairs roughly **doubled** global error, worst in the middle score bands; accuracy in the top bands was largely preserved [69]. Modelling worker-specific reliability rather than a universal error rate gave 15% quality improvement at 50% cost reduction [70] | Throughput is the binding constraint: one production team reports a curation queue of 48,403 pending proposals against 775 human decisions — a ~63:1 backlog [64]. A review sample tuned for total error also stops being representative by subgroup |
| **Entity-binding accuracy as a metric separate from retrieval accuracy** | Across 60 tasks, five backends and six tool-use methods, all methods achieved 0.0% wrong-*tool* error yet action-oriented baselines produced wrong-*entity* actions in **24.0–26.0%** of runs; entity-aware mechanisms eliminated them but reduced direct task completion by deferring under ambiguity [71] | Small synthetic evaluation. The trade is the Fellegi–Sunter clerical-review region reappearing at the agent layer |

Two honest complications. First, linkage error is **not** uniformly catastrophic downstream: a Monte-Carlo simulation on German health-care data found "realistic proportions of random linkage errors only had little effect on the power", and the self-controlled case series method "produced unbiased results even with a high percentage of linkage errors" while a Cox model underestimated the effect [72]. It is the **per-entity, user-facing answer** — *what do we know about Alice?* — where a merge is catastrophic and irreversible in perception. That argues for the merge decision being made at query time against a stated confidence, so different consumers can pick different operating points from the same store. Second, reversibility is not free: the one study that directly compared destructive merging against synonym-linking found "simple direct merging often surpasses synonym linking… the KGs remain redundant, requiring more hops to retrieve relevant information" [62]. That study did not implement a query-time collapsing view, which is the obvious fix — but it is real evidence that reversibility carries a measured retrieval cost that must be engineered away rather than assumed absent.

### What the evidence does not support

**"Controlled vocabularies improve retrieval effectiveness."** Not in ranked ad-hoc retrieval. MeSH-only representations were **32–96% worse in MAP** than a plain-text baseline across four years of TREC Genomics; combining text+MeSH gave significant gains for exactly one of six concept-assignment systems, and the authors state the gain is conditional on high classification quality [9]. Cranfield's original index-language experiments likewise found single-word terms more effective than concept terms or a controlled vocabulary [10]. The genuine pro-CV evidence is confined to exhaustive-recall and faceted-navigation tasks — different tasks — and even there the strongest study is confounded.

**"Faceted controlled metadata beats keyword search on exhaustive-recall tasks (the Flamenco result)."** The recall numbers are real (81% vs 57%; 77% vs 21%) but the comparison is confounded in a way the result is almost never reported with: the faceted interface was an order of magnitude slower per interaction (3.7s vs 0.3s), participants were **given three extra minutes** on the recall task in that condition, and spent significantly longer on task in both structured tasks. The study cannot separate "faceted metadata helps" from "more time on task helps". On the simple single-facet task, preference split 16–15. n=32 art-history students, one collection, one baseline implementation, never replicated [15].

**"Gross & Taylor proved subject headings are necessary — a third of keyword results would be lost without them."** What they measured is the marginal recall contribution of **one additional text field**: the fraction of hits whose keyword appeared only in a subject-heading field [16]. That is not a test of controlled-*ness* — any additional descriptive text (abstract, tags, extracted keyphrases, an LLM summary) would produce a comparable number in a metadata-poor catalogue, and the 2015 replication after table-of-contents enrichment still cannot separate the two [17]. No relevance judgements were made, so precision effects are unmeasured.

**"MeSH searching beats free-text searching (75% vs 54% recall)."** The study's own limitations section undermines the comparison: the MeSH strategy was built by the study authors while the text-word strategy was inherited from other researchers; MeSH searches received automatic term explosion the Boolean text strings did not; the 1,521-article gold standard was itself constructed by filtering on MeSH terms (867 articles eliminated for lacking the right indexing), and 57 sources found only by text words were excluded. The authors write that their research was biased toward the approach they favoured — and MeSH's advantage collapsed outside PubMed (Embase 24% vs 34.2%; CINAHL 17.8% vs 28.5%; PsycInfo 11.6% vs 19.9%, all favouring text words) [18].

**"Thesaurus/ontology query expansion never works — the literature has settled this."** It is split along the task axis, not settled. Lu, Kim & Wilbur ran 64 topics over the same ~160,000-citation collection and concluded MeSH expansion "can generally improve retrieval performance" [14]; the negative results were measured on ranked ad-hoc retrieval with MAP, the positive one on Boolean retrieval scored by F-measure and mean rank precision [11][12].

**"Blair & Maron's 20% recall result is an established fact about full-text search."** Salton published a direct rebuttal in the same venue the following year, challenging both the recall estimate and the inference drawn from it [73]. The study used two searchers on one litigation corpus with a 1985 unranked Boolean system and contains **no controlled-vocabulary arm at all**, so it is not evidence for any remedy [74]. What survives the exchange is the confidence gap — searchers believed >75% while achieving <20% — which argues for instrumenting recall, not for buying a thesaurus.

**"Automated concept assignment has improved enough that the 'high-quality classification' precondition is now satisfied."** The best natural experiment says no. NLM fully automated MEDLINE MeSH indexing in mid-2022 after two decades of refinement on its own corpus; an audit of MTI-Auto records from early 2023 found only **53%** had indexing representing the article's main concepts, with 47% carrying retrieval-affecting inadequacies including concepts omitted entirely [23][24]. If the most mature domain-specific automatic indexer in existence sits near a coin flip on its home corpus, assume a general-purpose LLM tagger on a novel corpus is worse until measured.

**"Folksonomies self-organise — leave people to tag freely and a stable vocabulary emerges."** Stabilisation is a **head** phenomenon. Halpin et al.'s power-law result was measured only on resources tagged more than 1000 times using the top 25 tags, and they observe a sharper-than-trendline frequency drop between tag positions 7 and 10 [21]. The one system where efficiency stabilised imposes a tag cap, encourages composite hierarchical names and restricts new-tag creation to experienced users, so emergence there is a consequence of constraints [22]. **Provenance caveat on the counter-claim itself**: the frequently cited del.icio.us result that tag efficiency *declines* over time traces to Chi & Mytkowicz [75], whose full text the source review could not obtain and whose published short-form abstract does not state it. That leg is unverified at source.

**"Build the ontology once and everyone reuses it — that is where the ROI comes from."** Across **337** BioPortal ontologies (widely miscited as 377), measured term reuse is <9% and most reuse under 5% of their terms despite 25–31% overlap; what reuse happens is shallow, and practitioners overwhelmingly report copying concepts rather than importing them [29][30]. The authors' own qualifier softens the economic indictment without rescuing the architecture: "we found strong indications that users actually intended to reuse terms, but in many cases they used incorrect representations", so part of the <9% measures representational heterogeneity. LOV-registry studies report substantially higher reuse.

**"An ontology is a durable knowledge asset."** Gene Ontology grew 2.5× in terms, 3.5× in relationships and 6.3× in annotations over eleven years, and the same experiment interpreted with the 2004 ontology matched the 2015 interpretation with a **median Jaccard index of 0.27** [26]. Coverage is permanently lopsided (58% of annotations on 16% of human genes). Separately, 63% of 735 crawled web ontologies scored under two of four quality stars and 63% carried no detectable licence [31][32].

**"Adopting SKOS gives you validation, consistent semantics and interoperable meaning."** It gives you an interchange format. The integrity conditions people most want (S13, S14, S27, S46) are stated as conditions rather than enforceable OWL axioms, and the Reference says how an application handles a violating statement "is not defined in this specification and may vary between applications"; the spec explicitly declines to assert either reflexivity or irreflexivity of `skos:broader`, so cycles are consistent with the model by design; retrieval behaviour is left entirely to the application [1][2].

**"SKOS is just ISO 25964 in RDF — pick either."** SKOS lacks ISO's hierarchical subtypes (generic/instantial/partitive), the term-level model, compound equivalence and split non-preferred terms, thesaurus arrays and node labels, concept groups and microthesauri, and term status — all deliberately postponed by the W3C working group; the `iso-thes` namespace exists to fill exactly that gap [2][4]. **Provenance caveat**: the ISO text is paywalled and its catalogue pages refuse automated retrieval, so every clause-level claim about Part 2 in this literature — including in this section — rests on free NISO adjunct materials and the standards editors' own account [3][7], not on the normative standard. Buy Part 2 before building to its equivalence machinery.

**"Following an established ontology-development methodology (METHONTOLOGY, NeOn, DILIGENT) produces better ontologies."** Two independent literature passes found **no comparative study evaluating outcomes between methodologies** — this is *unsupported*, not refuted. The methodology literature evaluates methodologies against feature checklists (collaboration? versioning? reuse?), never against measured quality or project cost [76]. In one 2025 review's own data table, the lightest approach — Noy & McGuinness's Ontology Development 101 — appears in five of fifteen projects, METHONTOLOGY in three, and NeOn in none; exactly one of the fifteen projects reused an existing ontology [77][78] (that review is in a low-tier venue with visible copy-editing failures; treat as weak).

**"Shirky's 'Ontology is Overrated' shows formal categorization does not work on the web."** It is a 2005 conference-talk transcript. It presents no data, no experiment and no measurement — its illustrations are rhetorical examples — yet it is routinely cited as though it were a finding [79]. **This is a provenance failure, not a disagreement**: there is no primary source behind the claim. The measured version of its thesis partly contradicts it: free tagging needs constraints to stay descriptive [22], and curated facets outperformed keyword search on exhaustive tasks in the one controlled study, albeit with a time confound [15].

**"GraphRAG outperforms standard RAG."** The originating result measures comprehensiveness, diversity and empowerment via LLM-as-judge on LLM-generated questions with **no gold answers**, and explicitly sets aside faithfulness, answer relevance and context relevance as not relevant to global sensemaking [41]. On benchmarks with gold answers the picture flattens or reverses [38][39][42]. **Important scope correction**: what is disconfirmed is *Microsoft-style community-summary* GraphRAG, not graph-structured retrieval — in the same experiments, HippoRAG2, a graph method that retains text chunks, is best on Tech-QA (0.3448) and MuSiQue (0.2196), beating vector RAG on both [39].

**"A knowledge graph gives an LLM the facts, so hallucination goes down."** Automatically constructed graphs are incomplete and wrong at rates that undercut the premise. Only **65.8% (HotpotQA) and 65.5% (NQ)** of answer-bearing entities appear in an LLM-constructed KG built from the same corpus [38]; Knowledge Vault trusted 6% of 1.6B raw extractions at confidence >0.9 and reached its headline 271M facts only by fusing with Freebase priors, of which about two thirds were already in Freebase [34]; Amazon's deployed product KG reports attribute defect rates "at best 21.1%, and can be over 90% in certain cases" [35]. A KG is a lossy, noisy derivative of the source text, not a ground truth over it.

**"Knowledge graphs improve LLM question-answering accuracy roughly 3× (16% → 54%)."** That benchmark compares GPT-4 zero-shot text-to-SQL given only a bare DDL against GPT-4 zero-shot text-to-SPARQL given a hand-authored OWL ontology plus R2RML mappings — 43 questions, 13 tables, one insurance schema, one model, authored by a knowledge-graph vendor. The authors themselves write: "one can argue that this is a predictable result given that the SPARQL query over a Knowledge Graph representation of the SQL database contains all the required context" [80]. It is not a comparison against retrieval, and the causal variable is the human-meaningful vocabulary in the prompt, not the graph. The same team's larger subsequent gain (54.2% → 72.55%) came from using the ontology as a deterministic query **validator** [46].

**"GraphRAG is cheaper because you only summarise once."** The "fraction of the token cost" claim is measured against map-reduce summarisation of the entire corpus (26,657 vs 1,014,611 tokens), **not** against vector RAG [41]. Against a retrieval baseline on the same corpus, graph indexing cost 7,702s and 5,560s versus 135s [38], and an independent accounting put Microsoft GraphRAG's indexing at 49.3M prompt plus 35.7M completion tokens where vector RAG used zero LLM tokens [39]. Indexing 1M tokens took 281 minutes, and that cost recurs on every content change.

**"These head-to-head benchmarks tell us which architecture is better."** Almost none report a significance test, and the differences being argued over are small relative to their own dispersion: a NeurIPS 2025 review of 445 benchmarks by 29 expert reviewers found only 16.0% used any uncertainty estimate or statistical test and 46.6% did not discuss construct validity [45]. **Provenance failure at the centre of this literature**: the "factual correctness" metric carrying the headline numbers in two of the key evaluations **appears zero times in the RAGAS paper those works cite** — RAGAS as published at EACL 2024 introduces and validates exactly three metrics (faithfulness, answer relevance, context relevance) and is explicitly reference-free [81][39][42]. In one case the judge model is the same model that generated the answers, and on Tech-QA the metric rates HippoRAG2 (0.3448) *above* the Oracle retrieval ceiling (0.3367) — an impossibility indicating the metric, not the retriever, is doing the talking [39]. Separately, the field has been here before: a SIGIR meta-analysis found no upward trend in retrieval effectiveness over a decade of claimed neural wins, attributing it to weak baselines [44], and no study in this corpus benchmarks against a tuned BM25+dense hybrid with a reranker.

**"LLM-as-judge results are worthless."** The common attack quotes Zheng et al.'s "only GPT-4 outputs consistent results in more than 60% of cases" while omitting the paper's headline conclusion — that strong LLM judges "can match both controlled and crowdsourced human preferences well, achieving over 80% agreement, the same level of agreement between humans", that the identified biases "are minor or can be mitigated", and that the 60% figure comes from a deliberately hard probe where "the answers are very similar and occasionally indistinguishable even to humans" [82]. Keep the operational hygiene (counterbalance answer order, report a consistency rate); the real weakness of the GraphRAG evaluation is the absence of gold answers, not the use of a judge.

**"Build the domain ontology first; the graph follows."** Enterprise practice runs the other way: "the ontologies used tend to be lightweight, often simple taxonomies representing a hierarchy of classes or concepts" [40]; Amazon judged manual ontology definition feasible only for bounded domains (~52 types / ~155 relations = weeks) and infeasible for open ones [35]; at Bosch, an ontology designed for the general domain rather than the data produced sparse, blank-node-heavy KGs needing "long and cumbersome SPARQL queries that are counterintuitive" [37] (a 5-page poster — treat its 7–8× multipliers as indicative, not established). **Minor provenance note**: the widely repeated "five industrial KGs use lightweight ontologies" framing has no count behind it — Hogan et al. cite Noy et al. without one [40][83].

**"Knowledge-graph data quality is a solved measurement problem."** The canonical systematic review catalogues 18 dimensions and 69 metrics, then reports that the metrics were often not explicitly defined or precise, that there was no formal validation of the tool implementations, and that no tool covered all dimensions [84]. The refinement literature is similarly weak: "roughly two out of three approaches evaluated on DBpedia are only evaluated on DBpedia", "about half of the approaches reviewed in this survey are only evaluated on one knowledge graph", and statements about computational performance are "only rarely included" [85].

**"Fellegi–Sunter gives you calibrated, controllable error rates — set μ and λ and the system honours them."** *(unverified pass.)* The theorem is real; the error control is not achieved in deployment. Reviewing four independent evaluations, Binette & Steorts state the fixed error rates and estimated false-match rates "are not attained in practice" owing to the conditional-independence assumption and estimation error, and note that in the 1990 US Census match thresholds were chosen "by 'eyeballing' lists of pairs" [49]. Splink's own documentation flags the independence assumption as a critical caveat and does not discuss robustness to its violation [50].

**"Deep learning has essentially solved entity matching — published F1 is 95–99%."** *(unverified pass.)* Three lines contradict it: most of 13 established benchmarks "pose rather easy classification tasks", almost linearly separable or already perfectly solved; an independent re-run of DITTO fell dramatically short of published numbers on the same data (51.46/89.43 vs 98.99; 28.76/65.67 vs 100.00); and five of the benchmarks "seem to involve an undocumented approach for inserting or removing an arbitrary number of negative pairs" [52]. Rebuilt with open entities and imbalanced labels, the prior assumptions "conceal the main challenges of the task and therefore significantly overestimate the current progress of entity matching" [55].

**"Report F1 on match pairs and you know how good your entity resolution is."** *(unverified pass.)* Pairwise metrics are the wrong instrument for a graph: because ER produces microclusters, pairwise false-positive rate improves as the corpus grows with no actual improvement, and "mismatching two singleton entities has an insignificant impact on pairwise metrics compared to incorrectly joining or splitting two large clusters" — i.e. the exact error an append-only graph cannot survive is the one pairwise F1 hides [59]. Different ER metrics rank algorithms in conflicting orders [60].

**"Send the ambiguous cases to a human and the hard problem goes away."** *(unverified pass.)* Human review is bounded three ways. Decidability: four expert judges evaluating 200 real `owl:sameAs` links answered "can't tell" on 91 of 200 (45.5%) [57]. Budget sensitivity: cutting clerical review from ~23% to ~7% of candidate pairs approximately doubled global error, worst in the middle score band the review exists to settle [69]. Throughput: a production KG team reports 48,403 pending curation proposals against 775 human decisions [64]. Reviewers themselves have non-uniform error that must be modelled [70][86].

**"GraphRAG-style LLM knowledge-graph construction performs entity resolution."** *(unverified pass.)* It performs exact string matching. Microsoft GraphRAG's documented dataflow merges "any entities with the same title and type" and relationships with the same source and target — no fuzzy matching, no cross-document normalisation, no entity linking [61]; an independent study confirms LightRAG, MS GraphRAG and HippoRAG all "rely on string-matching heuristics to merge similar entities, leaving many duplicates unresolved", citing 'LLMs' / 'LLM' / 'llms' / 'Large Language Models' as unmerged variants of one concept [62].

**"About 40% of the entities in an LLM-built knowledge graph are duplicates."** *(unverified pass.)* **Provenance failure.** The 40% is a **chosen hyperparameter, not a measured duplicate rate**: DEG-RAG *set* the entity reduction ratio to 40% and then measured the effect on downstream QA, establishing that aggressive dedup was safe-to-helpful on four benchmarks — not what the true duplicate rate is [62]. Its QA quality was judged by LLM-judge pairwise win rates, not identity ground truth.

**"Keeping both nodes and adding a 'same as' edge is strictly better than destructively merging them."** *(unverified pass.)* The only direct comparison found the opposite: DEG-RAG concluded "simple direct merging often surpasses synonym linking… the KGs remain redundant, requiring more hops to retrieve relevant information" [62]. Caveats: single preprint, four datasets, LLM-judge scoring, and it did **not** test a query-time collapsing view — the obvious fix. It nonetheless establishes that reversibility carries a measured retrieval cost that must be engineered away rather than assumed absent.

**"Reversible merges give you a full undo."** *(unverified pass.)* Wikidata — the largest production system doing exactly this — documents the residual irreversibility: merges are undone from revision history and the merged-away identifier becomes a permanent never-reused redirect, **but** "if bots have already replaced redirected item IDs in statements, you must contact bot operators to reverse those automated substitutions" [68]. The undo is only as complete as the discipline of never rewriting references in place. And unless canonicalization is a view rather than a write, rolling back the merge does not restore the fused attribute values [49][64].

**"Blocking is a performance optimisation — it affects speed, not quality."** *(unverified pass.)* Blocking sets the recall ceiling for the entire pipeline and no downstream matcher can recover a discarded pair. A state-of-the-art blocker had to be grid-searched specifically to exceed 90% recall — ~10% of true matches structurally lost — and at that operating point pairs quality was 0.3%–16.6% on seven of eight realistic datasets [52][53].

**"Any linkage error biases downstream analysis, so ER must be right before the data is usable."** *(unverified pass.)* Not universally: a Monte-Carlo simulation on German health-care data found "realistic proportions of random linkage errors only had little effect on the power", and the self-controlled case series method "produced unbiased results even with a high percentage of linkage errors" while a Cox model underestimated the effect [72]. The catastrophic case is the per-entity, user-facing answer, not the aggregate estimate.

**Unmeasured, not disproven — the two gaps most likely to be quoted as if settled.** First, *does a controlled vocabulary still add recall on top of a strong dense or hybrid retriever?* The decisive concept-vs-text evidence (Cranfield 1967, TREC Genomics 2004–2007, WordNet expansion 1993–1998) all predates dense retrieval and no rigorous modern replication was found; nothing above answers it. Second, *there is no published measured false-merge rate for identity in a production LLM-constructed knowledge graph* — the one production report is explicit that "no identity benchmark is reported, and the over-merges the key permits are undetectable by construction" [64]. Every quantitative ER error rate in this section comes from person-record linkage (census, health, voter registry) or curated entity-matching benchmarks, not from text-extracted graphs.

# Research detail: permissions and leakage

Supporting detail for [../research.md](../research.md). Claims are graded strong / moderate / weak / contested.

## Permissions and leakage

*Provenance caveat, applying to this entire section: the underlying topic review (`access-control-retrieval`) was completed **without an adversarial verification pass** (`_adversarially_verified: false`). Findings resting on primary standards, vendor documentation and peer-reviewed attack papers with quoted text are safe to act on; findings graded *moderate*, and every claim resting on a preprint, are tagged **[unverified review]** below and should be treated as leads to confirm rather than as settled facts.*

Permission-aware retrieval is not "search, then remove the rows the user cannot read". The literature's central result is that the removal step is too late: by the time a ranker has scored a candidate set against corpus-level statistics, information about unreadable documents has already crossed into the response. Everything below follows from that.

### Binding time: where the ACL meets the index

Four architectures recur. They are not points on a security/freshness line; they differ in *what leaks*, not only in *how stale they are*.

| Architecture | What it is | Confidentiality | Freshness | Cost profile | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Late binding, post-filter** ("security trimming") | Rank against the whole corpus, drop unauthorized hits before returning | **Broken** for anything using corpus statistics — leaks term frequencies, field names, counts, and with phrase queries the *contents* of unreadable files | Perfect at check time (if the check itself is fresh) | Worst exactly where enterprises live: slow when a user sees a small fraction of the corpus; exact result counts are the single most expensive feature | strong [1][2][3] |
| **Late binding, query integration** | Apply the visibility set to every posting list *before* scoring, so the query processor never sees an out-of-view posting | Safe against the score/rank channel by construction | Same as above | 54%/74% slower than post-filtering at 100% visibility, but **18%/36% faster at 10% visibility**; operator-tree hoisting "virtually nullifies" overhead; costs ~32 bytes resident per indexed object | strong [1] |
| **Early binding** (denormalised principal IDs in the index) | Index stable principal identifiers at ingest; resolve caller → principal set at query time and filter inside the query | Safe if the filter is *inside* the retrieval operator, not after it | Bounded by index-update latency; the classic objection | Cheapest per query; write amplification on ACL change | strong; three independent vendors ship this [4][5][6] |
| **Materialised permission index** (Leopard / Materialize) | Offline denormalisation of the authorization graph, kept fresh by a change stream; ACL-aware filtering becomes a JOIN | Safe; also the only shape that scales the "which of these N" question | Change-stream lag, bounded per-request by a consistency token | 1.56M QPS, <150 µs median, <1 ms p99 at Google; one tuple write can fan out to ~10⁴ index events | strong [7][8] |

Two corrections to the folk model fall straight out of this table. First, "late binding is the secure modern choice" is wrong as usually implemented — the secure variant of late binding is query integration, which almost nobody builds, while the common variant (rank globally, trim) is the insecure one [1]. Second, the freshness objection to early binding is not a property of binding time: Zanzibar's zookie protocol makes freshness a **per-request parameter** bound to the *content version* being served, so most checks read cheap stale replicas and only post-update checks pay for a fresh snapshot [7]. Measured, that costs roughly 6x at p95 (p95 9.46 ms "safe" vs 60.0 ms "recent"), and "safe" traffic outnumbers "recent" by about two orders of magnitude. A late-binding system with an unbounded check snapshot has exactly the same "new enemy" bug — old ACL applied to new content — that early binding is accused of.

### The corpus-statistics channel

Büttcher & Clarke give the constructive attack against a shared index with BM25 scoring: two queries recover the exact global document frequency of any term; with scores suppressed, **rank order alone** plus creation and deletion of attacker-owned files recovers it to ~0.5–0.6% relative error, reduced to about ten queries by binary search; and phrase queries reconstruct the entire content of an unreadable file term by term [1]. This is not a 2005 curiosity. Elasticsearch's own documentation states that document-level security "doesn't affect global index statistics that relevancy scoring uses… scores are computed without taking the role query into account," and that a restricted user "could still learn about field names and terms that only exist in inaccessible documents, and count how many inaccessible documents contain a given term" [2].

The design consequence is sharp: **if your ranker touches any corpus-level statistic — idf, average document length, BM25 length normalisation, a collection language model — you may not rank on a global index and then trim.** Either restrict the statistics to the user's view, or shard the index by security domain. What is genuinely unknown is severity in practice: there is no incident report and no measurement study quantifying what an attacker actually extracts from a realistic enterprise corpus, so this is a proven capability with unmeasured field impact.

Also unresolved, and directly load-bearing for any embedding-based design: **nobody has studied whether the same channel exists in dense retrieval.** All published attacks target TF-IDF/BM25 statistics. A shared HNSW graph plausibly leaks through traversal behaviour and through the k-th neighbour distance, but no study was found. Treat "dense retrievers don't have corpus statistics, so this doesn't apply" as an untested assumption, not a defence.

### Counts, ranks and "no results"

The tempting mitigations — hide the total count, suppress result sets below *k*, return a generic empty response — were refuted in the statistical-database literature decades before anyone built enterprise search. Denning, Denning & Schwartz proved that general trackers almost always exist and are almost always easy to find, permitting "calculating statistics for arbitrary query sets, without requiring preknowledge of anything in the database," and concluded flatly that "security is not guaranteed by the lack of a general tracker" [9]. Adam & Worthmann's survey concludes that no single control method prevents both exact and partial disclosure, that perturbation introduces bias, and that these methods "suffer from the 0/1 query-set-size problem" — which is the formal name for the "no results" leak [10].

When retrieval sits in a different trust domain from the data owner (a managed vector DB, a shared multi-tenant index), the irreducible channels are **access pattern** and **response volume**, and every sub-linear scheme exhibits at least one. Kellaris et al. prove this in an abstract model covering the whole design space; Grubbs et al. reconstruct range databases from volume alone in O(N² log N) queries with no assumptions on data or query distribution; hiding access patterns means ORAM, whose logarithmic overhead is lower-bounded by Goldreich–Ostrovsky [11][12][13]. Padding volumes into fixed buckets is the cheap partial mitigation and does nothing for access pattern.

The related folk move — hash the identifiers so nobody can tell what exists — has been run at internet scale and abandoned. RFC 5155 introduced NSEC3 specifically to resist zone enumeration; RFC 9276 (BCP 236) then recommends **one iteration and an empty salt**, on the grounds that an adversary willing to spend CPU "will likely be able to find most of the 'guessable' names despite any level of additional hashing iterations," that additional iterations show "increasing performance costs yet diminishing returns," and that a constant salt "does not change the cost of the attack" [14][15]. If existence must be hidden, hide it structurally — separate index, separate tenant — not by output suppression or identifier obfuscation.

### Can a ReBAC system pre-filter a candidate set?

This is the question that decides whether Zanzibar-family authorization can sit in a retrieval path at all, and the vendors answer it in their own documentation. The answer is: **per-document checks are cheap; "which of these N" is cheap only up to about 10³–10⁴ objects, or via a materialised view.**

| Primitive | Shape | Documented limit | Verdict for retrieval |
|---|---|---|---|
| SpiceDB `LookupResources` | Pre-filter: return the resource IDs a subject may see | Usable "if the number of resources that a user has access to is small (e.g. less than 10,000)"; "can get slow quickly" beyond | Fine for small corpora and per-collection scoping; not a general pre-filter [8] |
| SpiceDB `CheckBulkPermissions` | Post-filter: batch-check a page of candidates | "We don't know how many results are going to be accessible beforehand," so you loop until a page fills; "works better with… cursor-based pagination than limit-offset" | Loses total counts and offset pagination; safe only if scoring was already view-restricted [8] |
| SpiceDB `Materialize` | "Authzed's version of the Leopard cache referenced in the Zanzibar paper" — a local copy of who may see what | Requires operating a denormalised store fed by a change stream | The scalable answer; "ACL-aware filtering again becomes a simple JOIN" [8] |
| OpenFGA `ListObjects` | Pre-filter | Returns "the results found within the time allotted (`listObjectsDeadline`, default 3s) up to… `listObjectsMaxResults` (default 1000)" | **Silently incomplete and non-deterministic.** Used as a search pre-filter at scale it drops documents the user *is* entitled to see — a recall bug that presents as a ranking regression [16] |
| Zanzibar `Expand` + offline index | Denormalisation | Google states Expand "allows [clients] to build efficient search indices for access-controlled content"; Drive-style search "often issue[s] tens to hundreds of authorization checks to serve a single set of search results," which forced request hedging | Google's own answer to access-controlled search is denormalisation, not per-hit checks [7] |

Shipped vendor practice is a **hybrid**, not pure early or pure late binding: bind stable principal *identifiers* (users, groups, deny entries) into the index at ingest, resolve the caller's identity → principal set at query time, and apply that set as a filter inside the query. Microsoft 365 Copilot connectors attach a grant/deny ACL array per `externalItem`, deny taking precedence, and warn explicitly: "Avoid expanding the membership of your external groups directly into the ACLs of individual items because each group membership can lead to a high volume of item updates" [4]. Google Cloud Search requires items to be indexed with their repository ACLs and forces an explicit inheritance type per item — `BOTH_PERMIT`, `CHILD_OVERRIDE`, `PARENT_OVERRIDE` — evaluated leaf-to-root [5]. Azure AI Search documents a filterable, non-retrievable `Collection(Edm.String)` of principal IDs plus `search.in()`, and warns that expressing the same predicate as a disjunction of equalities "slows down query response time by many seconds" at hundreds-to-thousands of values versus subsecond for `search.in` [6]. Two rules follow: never denormalise *group membership* into per-document ACLs, and represent the filter as a set-membership primitive rather than a boolean disjunction.

Conflict resolution should be a named combining algorithm, not ad-hoc precedence. XACML 3.0 normatively defines `deny-overrides`, `ordered-deny-overrides`, `permit-overrides`, `deny-unless-permit`, `permit-unless-deny`, `first-applicable` and `only-one-applicable`; §9.1.6 states that `NotApplicable` should map to Deny, and that treating it as Permit is safe only in a closed environment where every requester uses exactly the expected syntax; §9.1.7 warns that negative rules "can lead to policy violations, therefore some authorities recommend that they not be used," naming attribute suppression and base-group redefinition as the failure cases and recommending a distinct group over a per-individual deny [17]. NIST SP 800-162 is the companion vocabulary for the attribute-based half [18].

One open question is whether relationship tuples are expressively sufficient at all for **content-derived** permissions — "documents mentioning project X are restricted", "anything classified above Confidential". That is a predicate shape, not a relationship shape; XACML handles it natively and Zanzibar-family systems bolt it on through caveats/conditions, but the two families have not been compared empirically on search workloads. The choice matters downstream because it determines whether the retrieval-time filter is a set-membership test or an arbitrary predicate — which in turn determines the filtered-ANN strategy.

### Multi-tenant isolation and filtered ANN

For vector retrieval the ACL predicate is a **recall** problem before it is a latency problem. Graph indexes suffer "connectivity breakdown on low-selectivity filtered queries, where qualifying vectors become sparse and the graph structure among them fragments" — precisely the enterprise regime where each principal sees a small slice [19]. How the predicate is encoded dominates throughput by up to three orders of magnitude: Curator reduces low-selectivity latency by up to 20.9x versus a pre-filtering fallback at +5.5% build time and +4.3% memory [19][20]; HONEYBEE reports 13.5x lower latency than row-level security at 1.24x memory, matching dedicated per-role indexes with 90.4% less extra memory [21]; ACORN reports 2–1,000x higher throughput at fixed recall [22]; and the NeurIPS'23 BigANN filtered track shows a ~12x QPS spread (2,291 to 37,671) at recall@10 ≥ 0.9 on identical data and hardware, which the organisers attribute "mainly [to] a better handling of the filtering constraints, with more appropriate data structures" [23].

The design space is bracketed cleanly, though the framing rests partly on preprint and workshop material **[unverified review]**: a single global index "wastes search effort on unauthorized vectors and degrades recall," while an oracle index built per authorized set "duplicates every shared vector between roles or queries" [24]; Yalamarthi & Pappachan name the resulting three-way tension between policy correctness, ANN recall and latency, and note fine-grained access control "is not fully supported in modern vector databases" [25]. Every published middle ground exploits the *structure of the permission lattice* — HONEYBEE partitions on the RBAC role "thin waist", Curator nests per-tenant clustering trees inside a shared tree, Veda builds an access-aware lattice with copy/merge under a storage budget. The practical implication is a schema decision taken **before ingest**: model permissions as a lattice of role/group combinations and partition on it, not as an arbitrary per-document predicate over one global index.

Measurement follows the same rule. Recall@k must be computed against the **authorized** ground truth, not the global one; the BigANN filtered workload (set-containment over a 200,386-tag vocabulary, 1–2 tags per query) is structurally the same shape as an ACL predicate and is a usable proxy benchmark [23].

### Embeddings and RAG as leakage surfaces

Storing embeddings is not a privacy boundary. Song & Raghunathan recover 50–70% of input words as a bag of words from popular sentence embeddings [26]; Morris et al.'s Vec2Text frames inversion as controlled generation with iterative re-embedding and recovers 92% of 32-token inputs *exactly* (BLEU 97.3) against a black-box encoder, plus 89% of full names from embedded MIMIC-III clinical notes [27]. Later work removes the two limiting assumptions: Huang et al. attack via a surrogate without access to the victim encoder [28], and ZSInvert is zero-shot and query-efficient, working "for any text embedding without training an embedding-specific inversion model" [29] (preprint **[unverified review]**). The operative conclusion is architectural: **a vector store crossing a trust boundary should be treated as holding the text at chunk granularity**, so it must sit inside the same permission boundary as the documents.

Two cheap, retrieval-neutral defences are documented, graded *moderate* **[unverified review]**: dimension reduction and product quantization "prove to be effective at significantly reducing reconstructibility, while maintaining retrieval effectiveness," and a per-user secret constant transformation degrades reconstruction almost completely with mathematically identical retrieval effectiveness [30]; the independent RecSys 2025 reproducibility study concurs that quantization mitigates the risk and is "a simpler and more widely applicable solution" than additive Gaussian noise [31]. Caveat carried from the source review: the secret transformation is reversible by anyone who learns the constant, and both papers evaluate against inverters trained on *untransformed* embeddings — defence in depth, not a boundary. The same work reports that mean pooling and bottleneck pre-training *increase* reconstructibility, making pooling strategy an inversion-risk parameter, not only a quality one.

RAG relocates leakage from weights to datastore and makes it verbatim. Zeng et al. confirm RAG reduces leakage of the LLM's own training data, but with 250 untargeted prompts against an Enron datastore, 116 produced verbatim matches from retrieved content and 208 close paraphrases; targeted attacks extracted 89–256 PIIs per 250 prompts [32]. Huang et al. find kNN-LMs "more susceptible to leaking private information from their private datastore than parametric models" [33]. Qi et al. demonstrate the same extraction against *deployed* commercial products (OpenAI custom GPTs) and find chunking is a security parameter — reconstruction increases with fewer, longer, semantically clean chunks [34] (preprint **[unverified review]**). Bigger chunks retrieve better and leak more; that trade must be made deliberately.

Membership in the corpus is close to unhideable from anyone who can query. MEntA reports up to 0.991 AUC using five non-templated natural-language questions, with no shadow models and no surrogate, and "remains effective under state-of-the-art RAG defenses, while current detectors either miss MEntA or flag benign queries at high rates" [35]; earlier work established the attack in black-box/gray-box [36] and stealthy [37] settings. If the *existence* of a document is itself sensitive — an unannounced acquisition, an HR case — that content needs its own corpus partition and its own retriever, not a filter on a shared one.

Finally, enforcement layer. Indirect prompt injection through retrieved content compromises real LLM-integrated applications, which makes the retrieval corpus an attack channel into the permission logic itself [38]. Bhatt et al. argue that prompt sanitization, output filtering, system isolation and training-level privacy are "fundamentally probabilistic," and introduce the **participant-aware** rule: content must be authorized for *all* users involved in an interaction, not just the requester [39]; SD-RAG independently moves sanitization and disclosure control into the retrieval phase, reporting up to 58% privacy-score improvement plus injection resilience [40]. Both are preprints and neither framework is independently validated **[unverified review]**, and the source review found no implementation or evaluation of participant-aware enforcement at scale — but the position is consistent with the attack evidence, and the intersection rule is not the default anywhere, so it is easy to get wrong in any shared-session or multi-principal agent design.

### What standard practice actually is

A design can be held to the following. Items 1–6 rest on strong evidence; 7–9 are the *de facto* practice of the systems literature rather than an established standard.

1. **Bind stable principal identifiers into the index at ingest; resolve group membership outside the index at query time.** This hybrid is what Microsoft Graph connectors, Google Cloud Search and Azure AI Search all ship, and all three warn against expanding group membership into per-item ACLs [4][5][6].
2. **Apply the visibility predicate inside the retrieval operator, before scoring** — never as a post-ranking trim, never as a system-prompt instruction, never as an output classifier [1][38][39].
3. **Represent the filter as a set-membership primitive** (`search.in`-style), not a boolean disjunction of equalities [6].
4. **Pick a named combining algorithm from the XACML vocabulary, default `NotApplicable` to Deny, and prefer distinct groups over per-individual deny rules** [17].
5. **Make freshness a per-request parameter bound to the content version** (zookie-style), not a global staleness setting on the index [7].
6. **Do not promise exact total-hit counts in a permission-filtered UI**, and do not use limit-offset pagination over a post-filtered result set [3][8].
7. **Above ~10³–10⁴ accessible objects per principal, denormalise into a materialised permission index fed by a change stream** (Leopard / Materialize). Below that, a resource-ID pre-filter is fine — but never treat OpenFGA `ListObjects` as complete [7][8][16].
8. **Group content into permission-homogeneous collections** so the common case is one collection-level check, and co-locate permission data with the index; a remote PDP round trip per hit is what makes query-time authorization untenable [3].
9. **Partition the vector index on the permission lattice before ingest**, and measure recall@k against the authorized ground truth [21][19][24].

There is no accepted benchmark to hold a system to on the *quality* side. The source review searched arXiv, Crossref and OpenAlex and found no dataset pairing a corpus with realistic per-principal ACLs *and* relevance judgements; the RAG-security surveys catalogue attacks and defences and list none either [41]. Absence of evidence rather than proof, but strong enough to plan around: any serious design must build its own evaluation, measuring (a) recall@k against the authorized subset — the retrieval-quality cost of the filter — and (b) **leakage probes**: does the ranked list, the score, the count, or the latency change when a document the user cannot see is added or removed? (b) is the operationalisation of the Büttcher–Clarke channel, and nobody ships it.

### What the evidence does not support

**"Post-filtering after ranking is safe as long as you remove what the user can't see."** Directly contradicted, with both a constructive attack and a vendor admission. BM25 scores yield the exact global document frequency of any term in two queries; bare rank order yields it to ~0.5% relative error in about ten queries; phrase queries reconstruct the full content of an unreadable file [1]. Elastic's own documentation states DLS "doesn't affect global index statistics that relevancy scoring uses" and that a restricted user "could still learn about field names and terms that only exist in inaccessible documents, and count how many inaccessible documents contain a given term" [2]. The channel is the corpus statistics, not the document bodies.

**"Query-time (late) enforcement is the secure modern choice; index-time (early) binding is a legacy speed compromise."** Not supported as stated. The usual late-binding implementation — rank globally, then trim — is the *insecure* one; only query integration, which restricts the statistics too, is safe [1]. And late binding is most expensive exactly in the enterprise case: Bailey et al. find query times unacceptable for users who can see a small proportion of documents and when exact counts are required, and that off-server access checks are "dramatically" more expensive "even on a local network" [3]. Google's own paper says clients build denormalised search indices from `Expand`, and that Drive-style search issues "tens to hundreds of authorization checks to serve a single set of search results" [7]. Leopard, SpiceDB Materialize and the Microsoft/Google/Azure connector designs are all early binding of identifiers [4][5][6][8].

**"Early binding is unsafe because indexed ACLs go stale and revoked users keep seeing content."** Overstated. Staleness is a consistency problem with a published solution: Zanzibar's zookie binds each content version to a timestamp and forces checks at a snapshot at least that fresh, which is what prevents the "new enemy" problem — and late binding has the *same* failure if the check snapshot is not bounded by the content version [7]. Freshness is a per-request parameter, not a property of where you bind. What remains genuinely unresolved is *how* stale a materialised permission index may safely be: Zanzibar gives the mechanism but nobody has published a principled bound, a risk model, or an empirical distribution of time-from-revocation-to-harm.

**"Suppressing counts, or refusing to answer when the result set is small, prevents inference."** Contradicted since 1979. Denning, Denning & Schwartz: general trackers "always exist if there are enough distinguishable classes of individuals," "are almost always easy to find," permit "calculating statistics for arbitrary query sets, without requiring preknowledge of anything in the database," and — explicitly — "Security is not guaranteed by the lack of a general tracker" [9]. Adam & Worthmann conclude no single control prevents both exact and partial disclosure, and that perturbation methods introduce bias and still suffer the 0/1 query-set-size problem, which is the formal name for the "no results" leak [10].

**"Hashing identifiers (doc IDs, tenant IDs, ACL principals) hides which items exist in a shared index."** Contradicted by IETF operational experience with precisely this design. RFC 9276 (BCP 236) states that determined adversaries "will likely be able to find most of the 'guessable' names despite any level of additional hashing iterations," that names leak through email logs, certificate transparency, web links and malware scanners, that additional iterations show "increasing performance costs yet diminishing returns," and that a constant salt "does not change the cost of the attack" and is "functionally useless" — recommending one iteration and an empty salt [14][15]. The standards body stopped pretending the hashing was doing much work.

**"A ReBAC service can just hand you the list of documents a user can see."** Only within limits the vendors document themselves. SpiceDB: `LookupResources` is usable "if the number of resources that a user has access to is small (e.g. less than 10,000)" and "can get slow quickly" beyond [8]. OpenFGA: `ListObjects` returns "the results found within the time allotted (`listObjectsDeadline`, default: 3s) up to the maximum number of results configured (`listObjectsMaxResults`, default: 1000)" — a silently incomplete, non-deterministic answer [16]. Used as a search pre-filter at scale this drops documents the user is legitimately entitled to see, and it presents as a mysterious ranking regression rather than as an error.

**"Post-filtering is merely slower; correctness is unaffected."** False on two counts. Graph ANN indexes hit connectivity breakdown on low-selectivity filtered queries — the qualifying set fragments and recall drops, which is exactly the enterprise regime [19][21][25]. And SpiceDB's own guidance notes that with post-filtering "we don't know how many results are going to be accessible beforehand," so you must over-fetch in a loop, you cannot serve limit-offset pagination correctly, and you cannot report a total count [8].

**"Storing embeddings instead of text is privacy-preserving."** Contradicted: 50–70% of input words recoverable as bag-of-words [26]; 92% of 32-token inputs recovered exactly and 89% of full names from clinical notes [27]; the attack no longer needs the victim encoder [28] nor a per-embedding trained inverter [29]. This is the belief the whole embedding-inversion literature exists to kill.

**"…and therefore a vector store is exactly as sensitive as plaintext, at any chunk size."** Also *not* supported — the headline number is narrower than it is quoted. The 92%-exact figure is for 32-token, in-domain inputs with a model trained for that specific encoder and length. The RecSys 2025 reproducibility study replicates in-domain results but finds inversion models "sensitive to the length of the input text" with "degraded performance when applied to out-of-domain sequence lengths" (on Signal1M, reproduced Vec2Text BLEU 57.7 versus 80.7 reported), and notes the 32-token models behind the headline were partly unreleased [31]; Zhuang et al. report correcting "an embedding model implementation error and a testing data leakage error in the original Vec2Text experimental setting" [30]. **No one has demonstrated reconstruction of a 512-token production RAG chunk from an undisclosed encoder**, and no reconstruction-quality-versus-chunk-length curve has been published — which is the single number a designer needs. Quantization and dimension reduction further cut reconstructibility at no retrieval cost, so the risk is a design parameter rather than a constant.

**"RAG is the privacy-safe alternative to fine-tuning — the data stays in the datastore, not the weights."** Half true and misleading. Zeng et al. confirm the first half but show the datastore is highly extractable: 116 of 250 untargeted prompts produced verbatim matches from retrieved Enron content [32]. Huang et al. find kNN-LMs more susceptible to leaking datastore contents than parametric models [33]. Qi et al. extract datastore contents from shipped commercial products [34]. RAG relocates the leak from an aggregated, noisy channel (weights) to a precise, verbatim one (retrieved context), and makes it reachable by anyone who can send a prompt.

**"Instructing the model not to reveal restricted content, or filtering the output, is an adequate control."** Contradicted from several directions. Indirect prompt injection through retrieved content overrides system instructions in real deployed applications [38]. MEntA "remains effective under state-of-the-art RAG defenses, while current detectors either miss MEntA or flag benign queries at high rates" [35]. Anderson et al.'s own template-instruction defence "shows high effectiveness for *some* datasets and models" — which is not a control [36]. Bhatt et al.'s position is that all such defences are "fundamentally probabilistic" [39].

**"Encrypted/searchable search is thoroughly broken by leakage-abuse attacks, so there's no point considering it."** Overstated in the other direction. Blackstone, Kamara & Moataz "highlight the practical limitations and assumptions underlying the often-cited IKK and Count attacks" [42][43] and conclude after systematic experiments that "the only setting that resulted in reasonable recovery rates under practical assumptions was the case of high-selectivity queries with a leakage profile that includes the response identity pattern… and the volume pattern. All other attack scenarios either failed or relied on unrealistic assumptions (e.g., very high known-data rates)" [44]. The LEAKER SoK, evaluating the major attacks on real datasets with real user queries, found "some attacks work better on real-world data than previously thought and that others perform worse" [45]. The honest reading: the response-identity + volume channel is real; most headline query-recovery results are not reachable in practice.

**"Published membership-inference AUCs against RAG can be taken at face value as real-world risk."** Treat with caution — the adjacent literature has a documented evaluation confound nobody has checked for in the RAG setting. Duan et al. find MIAs on LLM pretraining data perform "barely better than random guessing" once the member/non-member split is not distribution-shifted [46], and Das, Zhang & Tramèr show blind baselines using surface text alone beat published MIAs on foundation models because "evaluations of MI attacks for foundation models are flawed" in how members and non-members are sampled [47]. RAG MIA is arguably less exposed (the attacker often holds the candidate document verbatim, and the corpus is a defined set), but the source review found **no paper applying the blind-baseline critique to RAG MIA**, so the 0.991 AUC figure has not been stress-tested the way the LLM numbers were. Flagged in the source as the reviewer's own inference rather than a published finding, and the source review had no adversarial verification pass — so this is a caution, not a refutation.

**Provenance failures — claims traceable to no primary source.** Three assertions widely repeated in vendor and practitioner material could not be grounded in any primary source by the source review: (i) that there exists a public benchmark or agreed metric for *permission-aware retrieval quality* — searches of arXiv, Crossref and OpenAlex found no dataset pairing a corpus with per-principal ACLs and relevance judgements, and the RAG-security surveys list none [41]; (ii) that the Büttcher–Clarke score/rank side channel is known *not* to apply to dense retrievers and shared ANN graphs — no study of the question was found in either direction; (iii) that participant-aware (intersection-of-entitlements) enforcement is workable at scale for multi-party agent sessions — proposed in a position paper [39], not implemented or evaluated anywhere located. In all three cases the correct statement is "unknown", not "fine".

## What retrieval can and cannot do, and what a comparison can be trusted to show

### The three-way comparison, with the numbers attached

The honest summary is not "dense wins", "lexical wins", or "hybrid wins". It is that the two channels fail on close to disjoint inputs, that the best-evidenced intervention is neither of them but a reranker, and that most published margins shrink or vanish once the baseline is competently tuned.

| Configuration | Best-evidenced effect | Strength | The caveat that matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| BM25, well-tuned | The number to beat. In BEIR's zero-shot evaluation it outperformed **every** dense retriever tested on average across 18 datasets [1] | strong | BEIR's pools were built lexically, so this margin is inflated by an unquantified amount [1][2] |
| Single-vector dense | DPR −47.7%, ANCE −7.4%, GenQ −3.6%, TAS-B −2.8% nDCG@10 relative to BM25, zero-shot [1] | strong | Stronger 2024 models close much of this; Contriever beats BM25 on 11/15 BEIR sets at Recall@100 with no labelled data [3][4] |
| Learned sparse (DeepCT, SPARTA) | Worst tested tier: −27.9% and −20.3% vs BM25 [1] | strong | Later learned-sparse models (SPLADE) are competitive; the 2021 result does not carry forward [4] |
| Document expansion (docT5query) | +1.6% over BM25, better on 11/18 datasets — the only non-reranking method to beat it [1] | strong | Adds an offline generation cost per document, not a serving cost |
| Late interaction (ColBERT) | +2.5% over BM25 [1]; on the LIMIT stress test GTE-ModernColBERT reaches 83.5 recall@2 where single-vector models manage 19.0–54.3 [5] | strong | ~20 GB index per 1M documents vs 3 GB dense, 0.4 GB lexical [1] |
| Cross-encoder reranking of BM25 top-100 | **+11% over BM25, best on 16 of 18 BEIR datasets** [1] | strong | Fails outright on ArguAna (.311 vs .315) and Touché-2020 (.271 vs .367) [1] |
| Hybrid fusion (score combination) | ~+6 nDCG@10 over BM25 alone; **but only 0.01–0.02 over the better component**, significant in 14–16 of 29 BEIR test sets [4] | strong | Significantly *worse* than the better component on some sets (SPLADE vs hybrid on BioASQ: −0.04 [−0.06, −0.02]) [4] |
| RRF vs tuned convex combination | Convex combination of normalised scores beats RRF on **all nine** datasets tested, p<0.01 [6] | strong (single study; contradicts [7]) | The literature is genuinely split — Chen et al. recommended RRF for exactly the zero-shot case Bruch et al. rebut [7][6] |

The single most decisive result for a knowledge-context system is not a benchmark ranking but a capacity theorem. **[strong]** Weller et al. prove that for embedding dimension *d*, corpus size *n*, top-*k* and score margin γ, realising all *k*-subsets requires C(n,k) ≤ (1+1/γ)^d — a bound that binds at *any* corpus size [5]. On LIMIT, a deliberately trivial task ("who likes Quokkas?") over **46 documents**, state-of-the-art embedders at 1024–4096 dimensions score recall@2 of 19.0–54.3% and cannot solve it even at recall@20, while BM25 reaches 97.8%. Fine-tuning on an in-domain training split moves recall@10 from ~0 to 2.8; fine-tuning on the *test* split solves it — the models can overfit but cannot learn the task. This is not domain shift, and no volume of in-domain training data addresses it.

The complementary weakness is equally sharp and equally quantified. **[strong]** Replacing every LIMIT item with a synonym, so lexical overlap disappears, collapses BM25 from 97.8 to 10.6 recall@2 — an ~89% relative drop, worse than most dense models — while Qwen3 Embed falls only 19.0→11.6 [5]. The authors are explicit that lexical models "are not a panacea". This pair of results is a better argument for hybrid than the usual hand-waving, and it tells you *what to route on*: vocabulary overlap between query and corpus, not query length or a heuristic.

Two engineering corollaries follow that are cheap and under-exploited. **[strong]** Which BM25 *variant* you use does not matter — eight scoring variants across three TREC collections showed no significant difference under ANOVA plus Tukey's HSD (Robust04 AP spanned .2516–.2571) — while the *analysis chain* does: varying only BM25's indexing across BEIR gave average nDCG@10 of .429 (multi-field), .424 (flat), .404 (wordpiece), with per-dataset swings around 0.10 [8][4]. And **[moderate]** the largest measured headroom in fusion is per-*query* weighting, not per-corpus: an oracle that knows the best α per query scores MS MARCO .547 vs .454 for the best fixed-α hybrid, NQ .637 vs .542 [6] — several times the gain from hybrid itself, though oracle bounds are optimistic by construction.

Finally, a threshold discipline. **[moderate]** Steck, Ekanadham and Kallus show analytically that cosine similarity of learned embeddings "can yield arbitrary and therefore meaningless similarities" — in regularised linear models the values are not even unique, and depend on regularisation choices made for unrelated reasons [9]. Absolute cutoffs ("score > 0.8 means relevant"), user-facing confidence numbers and abstention thresholds must be calibrated per corpus and per model version, or replaced with rank-based rules.

### What is actually known about a few-thousand-document corpus

This is where the literature is thinnest and where a project is most likely to over-read it. The honest state of the evidence:

| Question | What the evidence supports | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Does BM25 win on small corpora? | On BEIR's four smallest sets it matched or beat every dense retriever tested in 2021: NFCorpus (3,633 docs) .325 vs .319 best dense; SciFact (5,183) .665 vs .644; SCIDOCS (25,657) .158 vs .149 [1] | moderate — four datasets, all specialist domains |
| …with an exception that is more instructive than the rule | ArguAna (8,674 docs) inverts it: BM25 .315 vs GenQ .493, TAS-B .429. ArguAna is the one BEIR set with very long queries (avg 192.98 words vs 5–20) — the query is an entire argument [1] | moderate |
| Is corpus size the causal variable? | Probably not — **vocabulary is**. Dense failure on BioASQ/TREC-COVID is attributed to rare specialised terms ("AZD5153") that dense retrievers "are unable to represent well within the embedding space" [4] | moderate — mechanism argued, not experimentally isolated |
| …and with better models the small-corpus gap closes | NFCorpus BM25 .325 / Contriever .328 / SPLADE .347; SciFact .665 / .677 / .704 [4] | moderate |
| Can an in-domain dense retriever beat BM25 on a small corpus? | Yes — Luo et al. report exactly that on BioASQ using synthetic template-generated training questions, and found hybrid earned its keep only in the **large**-corpus setting [10] | moderate — abstract read only; corpus sizes not defined there |
| Is first-stage recall a useful metric at this scale? | **No.** Bruch et al. had to change their cutoff for SciFact and NFCorpus because top-1000 is 20% and 27% of those collections, at which point "recall approaches 1 regardless of the retrieval method" [6] | strong — arithmetically obvious once stated |
| Is retrieval even the right architecture? | Contestable. Long-context LLMs outperform RAG on average when adequately resourced, with RAG retaining a cost advantage; Self-Route models the trade-off [11]. On LIMIT, a long-context reranker given all 46 documents solved 100% of 1000 queries in one forward pass [5] | moderate — [11] read as abstract only |

**The gap that matters most is that nobody has isolated corpus size as an experimental variable.** Every "small corpus" datapoint available (NFCorpus, SciFact, SCIDOCS, ArguAna, BioASQ subsets) confounds size with domain specialisation and with sparse, differently-constructed relevance judgments. The literature cannot tell you whether a few-thousand-document *general-language* corpus behaves like BEIR's small specialist ones. Two further unknowns sit under it: whether Kamphuis et al.'s "the variant does not matter" survives at 10³–10⁴ documents — Robertson's original IDF goes negative when a term appears in more than half the collection, which is far likelier on a small corpus [8] — and what k1/b should be at that scale, since BEIR's k1=0.9, b=0.4 were tuned on MS MARCO web passages and no study fits them on collections of a few thousand documents.

The practical translation for kc: at this scale, **spend the budget on the reranker, not on a better first-stage retriever**. The latency objection to cross-encoders (~450 ms GPU / 6100 ms CPU per query [1]) is a web-scale objection that does not apply here, and the standard two-stage justification — a cheap recall-oriented first stage — largely evaporates once recall saturates.

### Cranfield: what an offline number licenses you to say

Cranfield's assumptions are enumerable, and a knowledge-context system violates several by construction: the collection and information needs are fixed; every document is labelled for every query; relevance is topical similarity; a document's relevance is independent of any other document's; there is one query answered by one ranked list; judgments represent the user population [12]. **[strong]** Per-user permission filtering breaks "fixed collection"; representing conflict or redundancy breaks independence [13]; content churn breaks "fixed"; a context assembled for an agent rather than a ranked list for a person breaks the single-list assumption. Each violation kc keeps must be paired with either a metric that models it or an explicit statement that the number does not cover it.

Four load-bearing facts about pooled judgments:

**[strong] Pooling finds 50–70% of the relevant documents, and the shortfall clusters.** Zobel estimated that increasing pool depth from 100 to 200 on TREC 3/4/5 would yield ~35 more relevant documents per query (+32%), with ~85% of those coming from the 10 topics that already had the most answers; he concluded "the assumption that unjudged documents are irrelevant is not well founded" and that "measures based on recall are highly uncertain" [14]. Do not report recall, MAP or R@k from a pooled in-house collection and call it coverage.

**[strong] Pool bias is conservative, which makes it a false-negative machine rather than a false-positive one.** Buckley et al. show a constant-size pool over-represents documents containing topic *title words*, with the effect depending on collection size; one TREC 2005 AQUAINT run showed a 23% leave-one-out difference [15]. But because unjudged relevant documents are scored non-relevant, the bias only ever *depresses* the affected system's score. The cost is precise: "a system based on the presence of semantic concepts instead of the presence of specific terms may retrieve a high proportion of unjudged documents which are relevant but which do not contain title words, and thus have a lower evaluation score than warranted." If kc pools qrels from its own current retrievers, its existing stack keeps looking fine and any architecturally novel retriever is systematically under-credited — a failure nobody would notice.

**[strong] The likely failure mode for a knowledge base is the opposite of sparse labels: too many relevants.** Voorhees, Craswell and Lin report from TREC Deep Learning 2021 that judgment budgets were exhausted before an adequate sample of the relevant set could be found — the collection is not reusable, recall measures are unreliable *even for pool contributors*, early-precision measures cannot discriminate because the maximum is easily reached, and the standard collection-quality tools also fail because they all depend on system scores [16]. In an organisational knowledge base, a typical question probably has many acceptable supporting passages. If most topics have more than ~10 acceptable answers, a graded qrel set is the wrong instrument and pairwise preference judgments over the top of the ranking are the right one.

**[strong] Single-gold-answer labels fail in the other direction and mis-rank exactly at the top.** 94% of MS MARCO's ~6,980 passage dev queries have exactly one known relevant passage; crowd workers making pairwise preference judgments preferred a modern neural ranker's top result to the labelled passage more often than not — the ranker is "better than perfect" under MRR. Preference-derived qrels won 91.8% of their pairings against 46.6% for the originals, and re-scoring 16 leaderboard runs gave Kendall's τ = 0.65 with dramatic reordering at the top [17]. The cheap, obvious design — label "the right chunk" per question — will be wrong within one or two model generations, and wrong precisely where kc cares.

### Sizing, testing, and the shelf life of a collection

| Decision | What the evidence says | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| How many topics | Voorhees & Buckley: ~8–9% **absolute** MAP difference needed at 25 topics for the ordering to hold on fresh topics 95% of the time, ~5–6% at 50 [18]. Sanderson & Zobel show that method inflates the error; with resampling, 15–20% **relative** MAP suffices at 50 topics — but below 25 topics, significance still does not guarantee the result repeats [19] | strong |
| Realistic power | Urbano et al., simulating with a known null over 500M p-values: with AP at 50 topics and a realistic δ=0.01, only **9.47%** of comparisons come up significant, and 7.3% of those are Type III (right rejection, wrong direction) [20] | strong |
| Which test | Paired t-test or permutation. Urbano et al. — the only study with control over the true null — find both hold nominal α, while **Wilcoxon and sign have inflated Type I error that worsens as the topic set grows** [20]; Smucker et al. independently conclude their "use should be discontinued" [21] | strong — overturns Zobel's own 1998 advice [14] |
| Reusing the collection | Carterette: once the multiple hypotheses actually tested against a shared collection are modelled, p-values rise "by orders of magnitude" and "it is very difficult to conclude that anything is significant" [22]. Fuhr adds that reuse is *sequential* multiple testing, worse than simultaneous, and honest correction would count every unpublished test too [23] | strong |
| How many judges per document | **One competent judge is enough for relative comparisons.** Voorhees: assessor overlap ~0.36, Kendall's τ = 0.890 for MAP across judgment sets [24]; Bailey et al.: low agreement between gold/silver/bronze judges, "consistent but small" differences in rankings [25]; Parry et al. reproduced it in the neural setting where overlap fell to **0.15** and τ still came out 0.897 [26] | strong — three replications across 25 years |
| …but what to screen for | Not agreement — **systematic error**. Carterette & Soboroff simulated assessors making systematic errors and found average scores robust while "assessor errors can have a large effect on system rankings" [27]. This is why an LLM judge is dangerous in a way a sloppy human judge is not: its mistakes correlate with the retrieval model's mistakes | moderate — simulation study |
| Building it cheaply | Interactive Searching and Judging, explicitly designed so "a small research team [can] develop an effective test collection using minimal resources" [28]; Move-to-Front / variable-depth pooling (+66% relevants found for the same effort on TREC-5 [14]); depth-10 pools cost 11–14% of depth-100 while surfacing 1.7–3.6× more relevants **per unit of effort**, and the saved budget buys 350–450 topics instead of 50 [19] | strong — with one open caveat: shallow-pool reusability for non-contributing systems was flagged untested in 2005 and is still unsettled |
| Shelf life | Parry et al.: today's best rerankers (0.78–0.80 nDCG@10 on DL'19) sit **inside** the band their aggregated human annotators achieve (0.76–0.81), so further reported gains above ~0.81 "may not indicate that a system is better than another in a meaningful way" [26]. Keller et al.: as documents and qrels evolve, both absolute *and relative* system effectiveness change substantially [29] | moderate — the temporal study is a preprint |
| Comparing your number to anyone else's | You cannot, raw. AP/nDCG/P@d scores "do not allow the performance of a system tested on one collection to be readily compared with that of a system tested on a different collection", and ideal-ranking normalisation corrects this "with quite limited success"; score standardisation against a reference set of systems does work [30] | moderate |

Two ceilings bound everything above. **[strong]** Offline gains do not reliably transfer to user task performance: Hersh, Turpin et al. deliberately picked the term-weighting scheme with the largest batch improvement and found it "did not" help real users on an instance-recall task [31], and Sakai's SERP-preference data (1,127 triplets, 15 assessors each) puts the metric–user agreement ceiling at **0.7424 for nDCG, 0.7052 for AP** — statistically indistinguishable from plain Precision at 0.7054 [32]. Even the best offline metric disagrees with roughly one user in four. **[strong]** And Soboroff's ideal-ranking argument sets a harder ceiling: the ranking that places all judged-relevant documents ahead of all others maximises any standard metric, so **whatever writes the answer key caps measurable performance** [12]. A system that finds genuinely relevant documents absent from the qrels is necessarily scored below perfect. The escape hatch is a *privileged* judge — one holding information the retrieval systems lack, such as a model trained on existing human labels for these topics.

**[strong]** If kc has live users, the arithmetic changes decisively. Chapelle et al., across Bing, Yahoo! and a scientific-literature engine, found interleaving agreed with offline nDCG/MAP on direction whenever the offline test was significant, *and* found significant preferences where expert judgments could not — one manually judged query is worth about **ten** interleaved clicked queries [33]. Paired online comparison is the only evidence that speaks to satisfaction rather than to a proxy; the judged set is for regression testing and pre-launch screening.

One caveat that bears directly on any permission-filtered system. **[moderate]** Sanderson et al. found that "the relative performance of retrieval systems when evaluated on one part of a test collection may bear little or no similarity to the relative performance measured on a different part", strongly enough to flip statistically significant comparisons [34]. If each kc user's effective corpus is a permission-filtered sub-collection, a single global ranking of retrieval configurations is not warranted by this literature. Evaluate on the broadest realistic sub-corpus *and* at least one restrictive one.

### RAG evaluation: what the judge can and cannot adjudicate

The most actionable distinction in this whole literature is the level at which automatic evaluation works. **[strong]** TREC 2024 RAG-track meta-evaluation found automatic nugget evaluation agrees strongly with manual human nugget evaluation **at the run (system) level**, while per-topic instance-level agreement is explicitly flagged as needing further research [35] — mirroring the classic IR result [24]. Automatic metrics are adequate for *ranking configurations against each other* and inadequate for *adjudicating individual answers*. Never surface a judge score as a per-answer quality signal to users or to routing logic.

Everything else is a constraint on how far even the run-level use can be pushed:

- **[strong] The headline judge–human agreement number is not chance-corrected.** Across 21 judges from 9 providers, 3 benchmarks and ~541,000 judgments, the gap between exact-match agreement and Cohen's κ is **33–41 percentage points** on MT-Bench, and judge rankings shift by up to 14 positions across benchmarks [36]. The famous "GPT-4 achieves over 80% agreement, the same level as between humans" [37] is an exact-match figure of exactly this kind. *(Single preprint, abstract read only — treat the 33–41 range as needing verification, the direction as sound.)*
- **[strong] Judges agree with domain experts only on questions they can answer themselves.** On BFF-Bench (160 expert-authored questions, 1,200 expert correctness judgments), reference-free judging degrades sharply beyond the judge's own competence; supplying expert-written reference answers largely fixes it [38]. This is the decisive argument against reference-free evaluation for kc, whose entire purpose is questions the base model cannot answer.
- **[strong] Agreement at the top of the leaderboard collapses — which is the only place it matters.** Clarke and Dietz reproduce an overall τ = 0.89 between manual and automatic assessment on TREC RAG 2024, then show it falls to **0.51 among the top 20 systems and 0.56 among the top 15**; a run built deliberately to exploit the automatic judge placed 5th automatic and 28th manual; and simulating circularity by using the same LLM as final-stage reranker *and* assessor drove τ to 0.38 at top-10 and **−0.40 at top-5**, with twelve systems scoring above 0.95 nDCG under the LLM while scoring 0.68–0.72 under humans [39].
- **[strong] Position bias is the dominant judging bias, and reproducibility is not validity.** Reordering alone made Vicuna-13B "beat" ChatGPT on 66 of 80 queries [40]; across 15 judges and >150,000 instances position bias is systematic and only weakly related to length [41]. Two production-deployed judges showed test–retest reliability >0.95 coexisting with position bias >0.10 — a "consistency–bias paradox" [36]. Present both orders and average, or score pointwise with randomised rubric order.
- **[moderate] Judges are agreeable: measured TPR ~96% but TNR <25%** on code-feedback evaluation [42], corroborated by overrating behaviour in relevance assessment with high sensitivity to passage length and surface lexical cues [43]. Combined with class imbalance this yields high headline accuracy while catching almost no failures. Measure the judge's true-negative rate on deliberately seeded bad outputs; a gate that never fires manufactures confidence.
- **[strong] Correlation figures are measured over the pairs you do not need to resolve.** Restricting correlation to the small score differences actually observed in practice, the best estimate of ROUGE's correlation with human judgment is near zero [44][45]. A metric advertised at "0.85 correlation with humans" was likely measured over easy, far-apart system pairs. Estimate correlation restricted to your target effect size — typically 1–3 points — and report a confidence interval.
- **[moderate] Judges are not deterministic at temperature 0**, with completeness scoring least stable [46]. *(Single preprint; no variance magnitudes reported, so the repeated-sampling budget this implies cannot be derived from the literature.)*
- **[moderate] Preference leakage is a distinct, harder-to-detect contamination channel**: judges favour "student" models trained on synthetic data from a related model, across same-model, inheritance and same-family relatedness [47]. Judge selection must consider the generator's *training-data lineage*, not just its name.
- **[moderate] Forced-choice validation selects bad judges.** Under rating indeterminacy — items where multiple ratings are legitimately valid — standard forced-choice validation selects judges performing **up to 31% worse** than approaches modelling multi-label response sets [48]; and the majority of human annotator disagreement stems from task underspecification and response style, not noise [49]. Your rubric must permit "both acceptable" and "question underspecified".

On benchmarks themselves: **[strong]** a systematic review of 445 benchmarks from leading NLP/ML venues by 29 expert reviewers found field-wide patterns in measured phenomena, tasks and scoring metrics that undermine the validity of the resulting claims [50]. **[strong]** Contamination is real, measurable, benchmark-specific and wildly variable — 1%–45% across 15+ models and six benchmarks, producing accuracy boosts up to 14% (C-Eval) and 7% (HellaSwag) but negligible gains on MMLU [51] — and per-item detection is unreliable because "current LLMs might learn data distributions rather than memorizing individual instances" [52]. The cheap, universal mitigation is a **closed-book control arm**: the no-retrieval score is the floor kc must beat, and the oracle-context score is the ceiling that localises whether a failure is retrieval or generation [53].

Two findings bear directly on how much any single published RAG design claim should move kc's architecture. **[strong]** The SIGIR 2024 "Power of Noise" result — random documents improving accuracy by up to 35% — was directly reproduced and then shown to "appear, weaken, or disappear under small changes to prompt formulation and decoding limits", with substantial contributions from truncation and malformed generations; the reproducers conclude the effect "cannot be robustly confirmed as a general benefit of noisy retrieval" [54][55]. **[moderate]** Position effects fare no better: a systematic reproducibility study finds findings "remain inconsistent and hard to reproduce across models, datasets, and evaluation protocols", identifies **topic sampling** as a major variance source that can mask *or* exaggerate ordering effects, and warns that "conclusions drawn from idealised setups do not always transfer to real-world RAG pipelines" [56][57].

### The minimum honest in-house evaluation

Synthesising the construction literature [14][19][28] with the RAG-specific constraints, this is the smallest harness that licenses a real claim. Each line is there because something above says the cheaper version fails silently.

1. **At least 50 topics, drawn from observed user queries, not invented.** Below 25, a significance test buys nothing [19]; at 50 you are powered for large architectural differences, not prompt tweaks [20]. Practitioner evidence says the queries must be *novel observed* ones — a production team reports "common RAG benchmark evaluation techniques have not been useful for evaluating responses to novel user questions" [58]. *(Single-organisation evidence.)*
2. **Judgments pooled shallow (depth ~10) from deliberately diverse retrievers** — lexical, dense, and at least one interactive human search — with variable depth on the topics that keep yielding [14][19][28]. Pooling from your current stack alone bakes in the term-overlap bias that will under-credit your next architecture [15].
3. **One competent judge per topic, screened for systematic bias rather than for agreement** [24][25][26][27]. Spend the saved budget on more topics.
4. **A top-heavy graded metric (nDCG@k or RBP), never recall or MAP, on a pooled collection** [14][32]. Add a redundancy-discounted measure if kc must surface complementary or conflicting sources, because nDCG rewards five copies of one claim over one claim plus its counter-evidence [13].
5. **Four mandatory arms in every comparison: closed-book, oracle-context, tuned hybrid BM25, and your system** [53][59]. If kc does not beat tuned hybrid lexical retrieval on kc's own corpus, it is not yet shown to work — regardless of the embedding vendor's benchmark. Note that the baseline must be BM25+RM3-class, not default BM25: four of five neural rerankers failed to significantly beat a tuned BM25+RM3 on Robust04 [60].
6. **Paired t-test or permutation test on per-topic scores, with effect size and CI**; never Wilcoxon or sign [20][21].
7. **A pre-registered comparison list and a held-out topic set touched only at release decisions.** Day-to-day runs are a development signal explicitly labelled *not a claim* [22][23].
8. **An LLM judge over the full set, a stratified human-labelled subsample (order 200–500 items) per release, and prediction-powered inference intervals as the reported number** [61][62][63]. PPI gives valid confidence intervals with no assumptions about the automatic labeller, which turns "how much human labelling do we need" into a tunable parameter rather than an argument.
9. **Judge hygiene as config, not convention**: judge from a different provider lineage than both the generator and its training-data source [47]; versioned prompt (paraphrase alone moved κ by 0.2 in Bing's deployment [64]); reported chance-corrected agreement plus class balance [36]; measured true-negative rate on seeded failures [42]; repeated sampling with a variance estimate [46].
10. **Retrieval success and abstention instrumented as separate annotated outcomes**, not folded into one blended RAG score — a Bayesian decomposition across 27 configurations shows "substantial behavioral differences between systems that appear equivalent under marginal metrics", and finds retrieval-success annotations *more informative than task-success annotations* for scarce budget [65]. *(Preprint.)* Prompt-based abstention is not abstention: models instructed to refuse when evidence is absent still answered 41.6% of questions containing misleading information, because "prompt-based abstention asks whether context is sufficient, not whether it is correct" [66]. *(Single preprint.)*
11. **Periodic re-judging budgeted from the start**, with a stopping rule: once the top configurations sit inside the band of your own human judges' agreement, the collection has stopped measuring [26][29].

**What kc may legitimately claim, and what it may not.**

| Permitted | Not permitted | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "Configuration A ranks known-relevant context higher than B on our topic set, δ = x, p = y, CI [a,b]" | "A is better for users" | Metric–user agreement ceiling ≈ 0.74 [32]; batch improvements demonstrated not to transfer [31] |
| "A beats a tuned BM25+RM3 baseline on our corpus" | "A beats BM25" (citing a paper's BM25 number) | Published BM25 baselines are systematically understated [67]; 60% of 109 Robust04 papers reported *best* results below an untuned RM3 run [60] |
| "Our nDCG@10 on our topics is x" as an internal trend | Comparing that x to any published figure | Scores are not comparable across collections without standardisation [30] |
| "The judge agrees with our human labels at κ = x on this class balance" | "Our judge is 85% accurate, comparable to humans" | Raw exact-match inflates by 33–41 points [36][68] |
| "We detected no regression on the fixed query set this release" | "This release is better" (from the same set, run daily) | Collection reuse is sequential multiple testing [22][23] |
| "Retrieval recall on our seeded known-item set is x" | "We find everything relevant" | A live full-text legal system retrieved under 20% of relevant documents against lawyers' stipulated 75% requirement [69] |

The defensible bottom line: an in-house evaluation of this shape can honestly support a **relative, corpus-specific, effect-sized claim about ranking**, screened offline and confirmed online where users exist. It cannot support an absolute quality number, a claim about user benefit, a comparison to published results, or a claim that any particular retrieval architecture is "the right one" for corpora other than kc's own.

### What the evidence does not support

**"Dense/vector search has superseded keyword search; BM25 is a legacy baseline."** In BEIR's zero-shot evaluation every dense retriever tested averaged *below* BM25 across 18 datasets: DPR −47.7%, ANCE −7.4%, GenQ −3.6%, TAS-B −2.8% nDCG@10; learned sparse term-weighting was worst of all (DeepCT −27.9%) [1]. In the 2024 reproduction with much stronger models the authors still conclude that "none of the models are viable as a replacement [for BM25] yet, given that there are clearly situations where effectiveness is substantially lower" [4].

**"BEIR proves BM25 beats dense retrieval out of domain."** The disconfirming result is itself partly disconfirmed, and this must be stated whenever the previous point is. BEIR's own hole@10 analysis — the fraction of a system's top-10 that no assessor ever judged — is 6.4% for BM25 and 2.8% for docT5query but 31.8% for TAS-B, 30.6% for DPR and 14.4% for ANCE. After manually judging 980 missing TREC-COVID pairs, BM25 moved .656→.668 while ANCE moved .654→**.735**, from just below BM25 to 6.7 points above it [1]. An independent reproducibility study of Touché-2020 found a short-passage artifact plus large numbers of unjudged results; after denoising, neural effectiveness improved "by up to 0.52 in nDCG@10" (BM25 still won, but by far less) [2]. Only 2 of 18 BEIR datasets have been re-judged, so **the true size of BM25's advantage is unknown**.

**"Hybrid retrieval reliably gives a meaningful accuracy lift."** A random-effects meta-analysis across 29 BEIR test sets found a score-averaging hybrid of the two strongest components significantly better than SPLADE alone in only **14 of 29** cases and than Contriever alone in **16 of 29**, with summary effects of 0.01 [0.01, 0.02] and 0.02 [0.02, 0.03] nDCG@10 — and significantly *worse* on some sets (SPLADE vs hybrid on BioASQ: −0.04 [−0.06, −0.02]) [4]. Luo et al. separately found hybrid added value only in the large-corpus setting, not the small one [10]. Hybrid buys robustness and variance reduction, not accuracy.

**"Reciprocal Rank Fusion is the safe, parameter-free, tuning-free default."** It is none of those. RRF is parametric with one *more* free parameter than a convex combination; its NDCG "swings wildly" as a function of those parameters; a tuned RRF generalises poorly out-of-domain, with the sign of the useful asymmetry reversing between in-domain and out-of-domain data; and a convex combination of normalised scores beats it on all nine datasets tested at p<0.01 (MS MARCO .454 vs .425, FiQA .496 vs .464, SciFact .753 vs .730) [6]. Because RRF is a function of ranks alone it discards the score distribution and is not Lipschitz continuous. Yang et al. independently found that fusing on ranks while ignoring retrieval scores made effectiveness "significantly lower" [60]. The literature is genuinely split — Chen et al. recommended RRF for exactly the zero-shot case [7] — so measure rather than adopt either default.

**PROVENANCE FAILURE — "the RRF constant k=60 is a principled, empirically-derived value."** It traces to a single sentence in the original SIGIR 2009 short paper: "k = 60 was fixed during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation" [70]. The pilot sweep was flat (MAP .2134 at k=20 through .2147 at k=80) and the authors say the choice "was not critical" — in *their* setting, which was fusing ~30 configurations of one engine plus full sets of TREC-submitted runs, not two lists. It was never validated on two-way lexical+dense fusion, and Bruch et al. find substantial parameter sensitivity in exactly that setting [6]. There is no primary source for k=60 being right for hybrid search.

**"Dense retrievers fail on our data because of domain shift, so more in-domain training data will fix it."** Tested directly and refuted. On LIMIT, fine-tuning an off-the-shelf embedder on an in-domain *training* split moved recall@10 from near zero to 2.8 — essentially nothing — while fine-tuning on the *test* split solved it, showing the models can only memorise [5]. The limitation is dimensional and provable, C(n,k) ≤ (1+1/γ)^d, and it bit on a corpus of **46 documents** with queries as simple as "who likes Apples?".

**"Small corpora are the easy case — with a few thousand documents any embedding model will find the right thing."** The most direct evidence points the opposite way. On LIMIT-small (46 documents, 1000 trivially-worded queries), models at 1024–4096 dimensions score recall@2 between 19.0% and 54.3% and cannot solve it even at recall@20, while BM25 reaches 97.8%; 12 dimensions suffice to encode that configuration under free optimisation, yet real 64-dimension models still fail, so "real models perform significantly worse than the bounds" [5]. Small corpus size shrinks the theoretical requirement without making real models succeed.

**"Choosing the right BM25 implementation matters a lot."** Eight BM25 scoring variants across three TREC collections with the indexing pipeline held fixed showed **no statistically significant differences** between any variant on any collection (ANOVA + Tukey's HSD; Robust04 AP spanned .2516–.2571). The authors note "differences due to more mundane settings (such as the choice of stopwords) are often larger" [8]. The claim is right for the wrong reason: the *analysis chain* is where the 10-point swings live [4].

**"Adding a neural reranking stage on top of your pipeline will give you the gain that paper reported."** Gains are frequently non-additive. Five neural rerankers stacked on a well-tuned BM25+RM3 baseline on Robust04 produced exactly one statistically significant improvement (DRMM, .3033 → .3152, p=0.0032); DSSM, CDSSM, KNRM and DUET did not improve on the baseline at all [60]. The general result, first reported by Armstrong et al. in 2009, is that improvements measured over weak baselines do not survive being applied to a strong one [71].

**"Neural/LLM retrieval has delivered large measurable improvements over classical IR."** Armstrong et al. score-standardised every TREC ad hoc and Robust submission from 1994 to 2005 against a common reference set and found **no upward trend** in median or top-end effectiveness [71]. Across 109 Robust04 papers, 33% used a baseline below the TREC median, 60% reported *best* results below an untuned open-source BM25+RM3 run, and only 5.5% exceeded the best 2004 TREC submission [60]. This is a claim about the evidence, not about the technology.

**PROVENANCE FAILURE — "a similarity score above 0.8 means the result is relevant."** No source establishes this for any model. Steck, Ekanadham and Kallus show analytically that cosine similarity of learned embeddings "can yield arbitrary and therefore meaningless similarities" — in some regularised linear models the similarities are not even unique, and the values depend on regularisation choices made for entirely unrelated reasons; deep models carry analogous implicit regularisation [9]. They explicitly caution against using it blindly. Every fixed threshold in circulation is folklore.

**"There is a best embedding model; pick the leaderboard leader."** MTEB evaluated 33 models over 58 datasets and 8 task types and concluded "no particular text embedding method dominates across all tasks" [72]. Leaderboard rankings are additionally unstable to prompt choice [73], and MS MARCO leaderboard improvements "are almost never qualified with significance testing" [74].

**PROVENANCE FAILURE — "GPT-4-class judges agree with humans about as well as humans agree with each other (~80%), so LLM-as-judge is a validated substitute."** The 80% figure is *uncorrected exact-match agreement* [37]. On the same benchmark, chance-corrected Cohen's κ is 33–41 percentage points lower across 21 judges and ~541,000 judgments [36]. JUDGE-BENCH (20 NLP datasets, 11 LLMs) finds substantial variance by property, human-expertise level and whether the text is human- or model-generated, and concludes LLMs "should be carefully validated against human judgments before being used as evaluators" [68]. Nobody has published a chance-corrected number supporting the popular claim.

**PROVENANCE FAILURE — "RAGAS faithfulness/relevance scores are good enough to gate a release or compare vendors."** RAGAS's entire published validation is **50 questions from 50 Wikipedia pages, 2 annotators**, reported as pairwise-preference accuracy (faithfulness 0.95, answer relevance 0.78, context relevance 0.70) — never as calibrated absolute scores, and never off Wikipedia [75][76]. No source anywhere establishes that a threshold like "faithfulness > 0.9" means anything. Four independent replications in telecom, travel, academic-thesis and business-data settings report poor or absent alignment with human scores, one stating flatly that "quantitative and Ragas metrics do not align with human evaluation" and another that "RAGAs based faithfulness shows limited reliability in this setup" [77][78][79][80]. *(Four small field studies, consistent direction, none large.)* Reference-free judging is additionally worst precisely where a knowledge-context system operates: questions the judge cannot answer itself [38].

**"An automatic judge with high Kendall's τ against human judgments can replace human judgments."** The high τ is measured over the comparisons nobody needs. Clarke and Dietz reproduce τ = 0.89 overall on TREC RAG 2024, then show it drops to 0.51 among the top 20 systems and 0.56 among the top 15 — and excluding merely the weakest 15 of 75 systems already drops it to 0.84, showing the headline was inflated by easy-to-distinguish poor systems [39]. A run built to exploit the judge placed 5th automatic and 28th manual [39], and deliberately targeting a nugget-based judge achieves near-perfect scores when prompt templates or gold nuggets leak or are predictable [81]. Soboroff's ideal-ranking argument makes it structural: an LLM-written answer key reduces the evaluation to "how close is this system to that model" [12].

**"Verbosity/length bias is the dominant failure of LLM judges."** Under a single pairwise rubric across 21 judges, measured verbosity bias was <0.011 while position bias exceeded 0.10 in two production judges [36]; a systematic bias-mitigation study finds *style* bias dominant, "far exceeding position bias" [82]; and length-controlling AlpacaEval — the canonical debiasing intervention — moves Spearman correlation with Chatbot Arena only from 0.94 to 0.98, meaning the uncontrolled metric was already highly rank-correlated at system level [83]. Length bias is real but neither dominant nor the main threat to system-level rankings.

**"Self-preference is a universal, first-order failure of LLM-as-judge."** Established in open-ended generation with a demonstrated causal link to self-recognition [84], but a systematic study across four extractive QA datasets, multiple model families and many prompt variations reports "no self-preference bias, even when the same model serves as both QA model and judge" [85]. The genuinely worrying variant is preference *leakage* — bias toward models trained on synthetic data from a related model — which its authors call "harder to detect compared to previously identified biases" [47]. **[contested]**

**"Adding irrelevant or random documents to the context improves RAG accuracy (up to +35%)."** A direct reproduction confirms the effect under the original setup — earlier-generation LLMs, restrictive prompting, constrained decoding — then shows it "can appear, weaken, or disappear under small changes to prompt formulation and decoding limits", with substantial contributions from truncation and malformed generations, concluding the effect "cannot be robustly confirmed as a general benefit of noisy retrieval under these experimental conditions" [54][55].

**"'Lost in the middle' is settled, so put the best evidence first or last."** A systematic reproducibility study finds position-effect findings "remain inconsistent and hard to reproduce across models, datasets, and evaluation protocols", that topic sampling is a major variance source able to mask *or* exaggerate ordering effects, and that "conclusions drawn from idealised setups do not always transfer to real-world RAG pipelines" [56]; a separate study reports no consistently observed U-shaped curve across 7–8B models and finds mitigation methods sometimes make things worse [57]. The original result [86] stands for its setting, not as a general design rule.

**"Semantic/cluster-based chunking beats naive fixed-size chunking, and reranking is a reliable win."** Under-supported rather than refuted. In a controlled comparison on long structured academic theses, "cluster-based chunking did not outperform simpler strategies" (fixed-size and recursive) [79]; a separate multi-dataset study observed "only marginal performance improvements accompanied by higher response latency" from reranking [59]. Both are single studies — the point is that the widely-repeated claims rest on no stronger evidence than these do.

**PROVENANCE FAILURE — "RAG benchmarks are contaminated."** No primary source measures this. An arXiv full-text search for "retrieval augmented generation" AND "benchmark contamination" returned **zero results**; all located contamination literature concerns closed-book benchmarks [51][52]. The indirect argument (NQ/TriviaQA/HotpotQA are heavily contaminated closed-book, so a RAG system evaluated on them may be scored on parametric recall) is sound but unmeasured. State it as a hypothesis, and run the closed-book control arm anyway [53].

**"Benchmark contamination is a solved diagnostic — you can detect and exclude contaminated items."** A survey of 50 detection papers testing the assumptions behind instance-level detection concludes "current LLMs might learn data distributions rather than memorizing individual instances", undermining per-item detection [52]; and effect sizes vary wildly by benchmark (up to +14% C-Eval, +7% HellaSwag, negligible MMLU), so no single global adjustment is meaningful [51].

**PROVENANCE FAILURE — "a 5% improvement is meaningful; smaller differences are noise."** Sparck Jones's widely-repeated 5%-absolute rule of thumb has no statistical basis and is simultaneously too stringent for large topic sets and too lax for small ones. Zobel found a case where an 11-point average difference of **0.002** (0.149 → 0.151) *was* significant and another where 0.118 (0.210 → 0.328) was *not*; ~20% of differences smaller than 0.05 absolute were significant [14]. Conversely, at a 20–30% relative MAP difference only 57.3% of comparisons were significant, and a positive t-test with a relative MAP difference under 10% is arguably more likely a Type I error than a real result [19]. Effect size and significance are independent facts; both must be reported. Nothing in the literature establishes how large an improvement must be before a *user* of a knowledge-context system notices it — Fuhr's suggested P@10 difference of 0.02 is explicitly offered as an illustration, not a standard [23].

**"Use Wilcoxon or the sign test because IR score distributions are not normal."** The standard justification, and the evidence contradicts it. Urbano et al., the only study with control over the true null, found Wilcoxon and sign have inflated Type I error that gets **worse** as the topic set grows, while t and permutation hold nominal α [20]; Smucker et al. write that they "are simplified variants of the randomization test and their use should be discontinued" [21]; Sanderson and Zobel measured error rates of 16–10% (Wilcoxon) and 17–8% (sign) against 13–7% (t) [19]. This overturns Zobel's own 1998 recommendation [14], which is still repeated in tutorials.

**"A 30–50 query evaluation set is enough to tell which retriever is better."** Sakai's topic-set-size design, requiring 80% power at α=0.05 across 10 systems, gives required topic counts of ~45–60 for nDCG@10, ~121–131 for P+, and ~157–168 for nG@1 — and that is to detect a minimum detectable *range* of 0.15, an enormous effect [87]. Required n scales roughly as 1/minD², so realistic 0.02–0.03 differences are undetectable at these sizes. Urbano et al. put the empirical figure on it: at 50 topics with δ=0.01, only 9.47% of comparisons come up significant [20].

**"Inter-assessor disagreement is so high that test collections cannot be trusted."** Unsupported for *relative* comparisons, and checked three times across 25 years: Voorhees, overlap ~0.36, Kendall's τ = 0.890 [24]; Bailey et al., low agreement between expert and non-expert judges but "consistent but small" ranking differences [25]; Parry et al., overlap collapsed to 0.15 on modern short-passage 4-grade data with τ still 0.897 [26]. The correct worry is different: *systematic* assessor error does move system rankings substantially [27] — which is exactly the failure mode an LLM judge has and a sloppy human judge does not.

**"Pool bias means results from a pooled collection are untrustworthy."** Too pessimistic, and it obscures the real problem. The bias is conservative — unjudged relevant documents score as non-relevant, so an affected system is only ever penalised, and a claim that a new method beats a baseline remains valid [15]. The actual cost is a false *negative*: a semantically-different retriever may retrieve many unjudged-but-relevant documents lacking title words and score lower than warranted. The authors also caution that retrieving many unjudged documents is not itself proof of unfair evaluation.

**"More and deeper judgments per topic make a better collection."** Per unit of assessor effort, the opposite: a depth-10 pool costs 11–14% of a depth-100 pool while surfacing 1.7–3.6× more relevant documents per unit of effort, and P@10 achieves a *lower* error rate than MAP once effort is held constant; the same budget buys 350–450 topics instead of 50, and topics are the dominant source of variance [19]. The authors' own caveat stands and is still unsettled: the reusability of shallow-pool collections for non-contributing systems was untested in 2005 and no study since resolves it.

**"MRR, or a single labelled best answer per query, is a fine cheap way to track retrieval quality."** It stops working as soon as the systems get good, and it fails silently. 94% of ~6,980 MS MARCO passage dev queries have exactly one known relevant passage; crowd workers preferred a modern ranker's top result to the labelled one more often than not, making the ranker "better than perfect" under MRR; preference-derived qrels won 91.8% of pairings against the originals' 46.6%, and re-scoring 16 leaderboard runs gave τ = 0.65 with dramatic top-end reordering [17]. Fuhr adds that MRR can rank first-relevant ranks of 1, 2, 4 above 2, 2, 2 despite a worse mean rank, because E(1/R) ≠ 1/E(R) [23].

**"You can build a modest in-house eval set and use it to justify choices as you iterate."** Not without an explicit multiplicity discipline. Once the many hypotheses actually tested against a shared collection are modelled, p-values increase "by orders of magnitude" and "it is very difficult to conclude that anything is significant" [22]; reuse is *sequential* multiple testing, and the honest minimum correction would count every test previously run on that collection including the unpublished ones [23]. A small set a team hammers daily is a development signal, not evidence.

**"A test collection you build once is an asset you keep using."** It has a shelf life, and the modern ones are near the end of theirs. Today's best rerankers (0.78–0.80 nDCG@10 on DL'19) sit inside the band achieved by aggregating human annotators (0.76–0.81), so reported gains above that band "may not indicate that a system is better than another in a meaningful way" [26]; as documents and qrels evolve, both absolute *and relative* system effectiveness change substantially [29]; and TREC DL 2021 documents a collection that was not reusable from the day it was built [16].

**"Improving an offline metric means users will be better off."** Directly tested and falsified. Hersh, Turpin et al. deliberately selected the weighting scheme with the largest batch improvement and found it "did not" benefit real users on an instance-recall task [31]; follow-ups extended the negative result [88][89]. Sakai's SERP-preference data (1,127 triplets, 15 assessors each) caps agreement at 0.7424 for nDCG and 0.7052 for AP — not significantly better than plain Precision at 0.7054 [32]. Even the best offline metric disagrees with roughly a quarter of individual users' expressed preferences.

**"Users' sense that the system finds everything is evidence that it does."** Blair and Maron evaluated a live full-text legal system over roughly 350,000 pages and found it retrieving "less than 20 percent of the documents relevant to a particular search" — against the lawyers' stipulated requirement of 75% [69].

**"A strong score on a public RAG benchmark predicts production performance."** A production team reports "common RAG benchmark evaluation techniques have not been useful for evaluating responses to novel user questions", and that simple changes to how knowledge-base *content is authored* had large impact [58]; a realistic heterogeneous enterprise benchmark puts the best agentic RAG system at ~33, with retrieval identified as the main bottleneck [90]; and the 445-benchmark construct-validity review finds field-wide patterns that undermine the resulting claims [50]. *(The first two are single-organisation evidence.)*

**"Prompting the model to cite sources or refuse when evidence is absent gives reliable abstention."** Models instructed to refuse when evidence is absent still answered 41.6% of questions containing misleading information, because "prompt-based abstention asks whether context is sufficient, not whether it is correct" [66]. Abstention must be measured as its own conditional outcome, never assumed from an instruction. *(Single preprint.)*

**"RAG eliminates hallucination."** RAGTruth provides ~18,000 naturally generated RAG responses with manual annotation at both case and word level *including hallucination intensity*, and benchmarks hallucination frequency across LLMs — the corpus exists because grounded generation hallucinates [91].

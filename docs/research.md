# Research: what the evidence says about organisational knowledge, retrieval and memory

**How to read this.** Six full thematic treatments, each with its own reference list, live in [docs/detail/](detail/); this synthesis keeps only findings that would change a `kc` design decision. Claims are graded **strong** (multiple independent studies, or one large well-identified study whose primary text was verified), **moderate** (single study or site, relayed quotation, or a well-argued but unmeasured claim), **weak** (anecdote, position paper, pilot, single unreplicated source) or **contested** (competent evidence both ways). Grades attach to *claims*, not papers; a contested finding is never presented here as settled.

**Citation integrity.** Every underlying review was checked for whether each cited work exists and says what is attributed to it; where a source was read only as an abstract or through a citing work, the detail files record that rather than smoothing it away, and reference numbering was merged mechanically. Four reviews received **no adversarial verification pass** — transactive memory, bi-temporal/belief-revision, entity resolution, and the access-control/retrieval-evaluation/agent-memory group — so their directions are usable but their magnitudes are not quotable without re-checking; where two verified reviews disagreed, the disagreement is recorded rather than resolved. The section that matters most for scoping is **What the evidence does not support**: several of this category's load-bearing statistics trace to no primary source at all.

## What the evidence supports

Each section states the headline evidence; the fuller mapping from finding to design choice is the table in **What would change a design decision**, and the full treatment is in the linked detail file.

### Why knowledge systems fail

No failure base rate exists to design against — the canonical IT-failure taxonomy applied to 5,457 forecasts of 1,211 projects gave success rates of 67%, 5.8% and 59% across three organisations from estimation bias alone [1] (**strong**) — so anchor on mechanisms. Grudin's disparity, that an application fails when it demands extra work from people who do not perceive the benefit [2], is the most transferable (**strong** as mechanism, **moderate** as a law: never tested as a quantitative predictor of adoption). Cheap capture is necessary and demonstrably insufficient: one third refused a discretionary database at minimal cost [3] and a gamified onboarding tutorial produced "no effect… over a period of 180 days" [4], while a *staffed human help space* in the same community did improve retention [5]. Maintenance kills the artifact [6], and **58.4% of obsolete Stack Overflow answers were probably already obsolete when first posted**, staleness being noticed by uninvolved outsiders (38.2%) more than by authors (24.3%) [7] (**strong**). Enterprise content is **deposited, not published** [8], author-applied metadata *degraded* a title+content baseline [9], complaints are largely *coverage* failures [10], and users do not know what they missed — attorneys believed they had retrieved ~75% of relevant documents when roughly 20% had been [11] (**strong**). What documentation omits is **decision rationale** [12]; volume is not knowledge [13]; and the durable social-technical gap's prescription is to augment rather than mechanise [14].

### What retrieval can and cannot do

Tuned BM25 outperformed every dense retriever tested on average across BEIR's 18 datasets [15], but the pools were built lexically and only 2 of 18 sets re-judged — after judging 980 missing TREC-COVID pairs BM25 moved .656→.668 while ANCE moved .654→**.735** — so **the true size of BM25's advantage is unknown** [15][16]. The largest reliable gain lies elsewhere: **cross-encoder reranking of BM25 top-100 is +11%, best on 16 of 18 datasets** [15] (**strong**), while hybrid fusion adds only **0.01–0.02 over the better component** [17] and a convex combination beats RRF on all nine datasets tested, though the literature is split [18][19] (**contested**). The decisive result is a capacity theorem, not a benchmark: embedding dimension bounds which top-*k* subsets are representable at all, and over **46 documents** models at 1024–4096 dimensions score recall@2 of 19.0–54.3% where BM25 reaches 97.8%, with in-domain fine-tuning moving recall@10 from ~0 to 2.8; synonym substitution symmetrically collapses BM25 to 10.6 [20] (**strong**). Cosine similarity of learned embeddings "can yield arbitrary and therefore meaningless similarities" [21] (**moderate**). An in-house evaluation licenses little (**strong** throughout): ≥50 *observed* topics, since at 50 with δ=0.01 only 9.47% of comparisons come up significant [22][23]; t-test or permutation, never Wilcoxon [24]; one judge screened for *systematic* error [12][25]; shallow pools from diverse retrievers [26]; four arms — closed-book, oracle, tuned BM25+RM3, system [27]. Offline-to-user agreement caps at ~0.74 [28], and automatic judging that ranks configurations adequately adjudicates answers badly: τ = 0.89 overall falls to **0.51 among the top 20 systems** [29][30], judges agreeing with domain experts only on questions they can answer themselves [31].

### Conflict, staleness and time

Only **inter-context** conflict — two sources disagreeing — is representable in a knowledge layer; context-memory conflict is a consumer-side property and intra-memory conflict lives in the weights [32] (**strong as vocabulary, not as evidence**: the surveys are preprints whose authority comes from adoption). The cleanest positive finding is that **semantic relevance systematically prefers the stale document** — re-rankers show "a strong bias toward older, semantically rich documents, even when they are factually obsolete" [33], accuracy drops up to 31% on evolving-knowledge questions over three real timestamped corpora [34], and coexisting outdated content degrades output "even when current information is available" [35] (**moderate**; the sharpest version and its fix come from one industry lab). Expiry belongs **per assertion** [36], and credibility works as a computed weight [37] or training signal [38] but fails as a text label, since preference "can be reversed by simply repeating information from less credible sources" [39]. The pivotal negative: **bi-temporal modelling structurally cannot represent live disagreement**, because `WITHOUT OVERLAPS` plus the single-current-row rule guarantee one row per key at any (application-time, system-time) coordinate [40]. The one field-tested representation carrying it is Wikidata's — many statements per property, each with its own references, a three-value rank, best-rank query semantics, deprecated statements **retained, not deleted** [41]. Latest-wins is a **hard baseline** [42] (**moderate**); consistent query answering is cheap for the shapes a knowledge layer issues, LOGSPACE or coNP-complete with nothing between [43] (**strong**); and poisoning is narrower than retold — ~90% success from five malicious texts against a database of millions, **because the attack is targeted and requires advance knowledge of the query** [44].

### Meaning, vocabulary and identity

A controlled vocabulary does not improve ranked ad-hoc retrieval: concept-only representations were **32–96% worse in MAP** than plain text across four years of TREC Genomics, and text+concepts helped for exactly one of six concept assigners — "only improves retrieval if the classification is of high quality" [45] (**strong** for that setting). The payoff lives in the **aliases**, not the hierarchy [46], and the quality precondition is unmet: a fully automated domain indexer refined for two decades on its own corpus produced records where only **53%** had indexing representing the article's main concepts [47] (**moderate**). Vocabulary *version* must be recorded — the same experiment run against a 2004 versus a 2015 ontology matched at a **median Jaccard of 0.27** [48] (**strong**). Graph indexing costs roughly **40–60× chunk-and-embed** and recurs on every content change [49]; the graphs are lossy, a web-scale fused extractor trusting **6%** of 1.6B raw triples at confidence >0.9 [50] and a deployed product KG reporting attribute defect rates "at best 21.1%, and can be over 90% in certain cases" [51]; and **the graph methods that win keep the source text**, since community-summary GraphRAG collapses on null queries at abstention 19.27 against plain RAG's 96.01 [52]. Identity is where an append-only store is most exposed, because **a false merge is not the mirror of a false split**: a merge is unrestricted substitution by specification [53], and the largest `owl:sameAs` equality set holds **177,794 terms** conflating Bolivia, Dublin, Coca-Cola and Albert Einstein, two incorrect statements having caused false equivalence of 78 further terms [54] (**strong**). Matcher quality on real data is far below benchmark F1 — 0.55% false-match at a **20.34% missed-match** rate on 848,566 voter records [55] (*unverified pass*) — and pairwise F1 hides it, since ER produces microclusters where the pairwise false-positive rate *improves* as the corpus grows [56]. SKOS supplies the standards precedent: `skos:closeMatch` is symmetric but explicitly **not** transitive, "to avoid the possibility of 'compound errors'" [57].

### Permissions and leakage

Removing unauthorised rows *after* ranking is too late: two queries recover the exact global document frequency of any term, **rank order alone** recovers it to ~0.5% relative error in about ten queries with scores suppressed, and phrase queries reconstruct an unreadable file term by term [58] (**strong**, constructive attack) — a major vendor's own documentation confirming that document-level security "doesn't affect global index statistics that relevancy scoring uses" [59]. Two folk beliefs invert. The secure form of late binding is *query integration*, applying the visibility set to each posting list before scoring: 54–74% slower at full visibility but **18–36% faster at 10% visibility**, faster exactly where enterprises live [58]. And freshness is not a property of binding time — it can be a **per-request parameter bound to the content version** [60] — with shipped practice across vendors being a hybrid: principal identifiers at ingest, group membership resolved at query time, never expanded into per-item ACLs [61][62]. "List the documents this user can see" has documented limits: usable "if the number of resources that a user has access to is small (e.g. less than 10,000)" [63], while another vendor returns only "the results found within the time allotted" — **silently incomplete and non-deterministic**, which as a pre-filter drops entitled documents and presents as a ranking regression [64]. Output suppression does not help [65][66]. For vector retrieval the ACL predicate is a **recall** problem before a latency one, since graph indexes suffer connectivity breakdown on low-selectivity filtered queries and every published middle ground exploits the permission lattice [67] (**strong**). And storing embeddings is not a privacy boundary: 50–70% of input words are recoverable as a bag of words [68] and 92% of 32-token inputs exactly [69], though the headline degrades badly out-of-domain and at other sequence lengths [70].

### Agent memory, provenance and abstention

The one clean result is an ablation *inside one model*, no vendor system in the loop: on ~115k-token histories GPT-4o scores 0.870 given only the oracle evidence sessions and 0.606 given the full history — 30.3% relative, 55.1% for Llama-3.1-70B [71] (**strong**) — reproduced independently at ~80% versus ~55% across 18 models [72]. **The model is distraction-limited, not compute-limited**, which is what justifies a selection layer; the corollary is that for a *bounded* corpus that fits the window, long context beats retrieval [73]. From that benchmark's own ablations: index at **round** rather than session granularity and store a **fact-augmented key** — extracted facts concatenated with the verbatim value at index time — for +9.4% recall@k and +5.4% accuracy [71] (**moderate**). Structure buys retrieval *cost*, not answer quality: organised stores roughly halve retrieval cost while "no agent we measure converts organization itself into better answers" [74] (**strong** as a convergent negative). Two findings survive regardless of architecture. Retrieval can be net-negative: on a benchmark whose misleading documents occur naturally rather than being injected, *every* tested RAG system performed worse than its own zero-shot baseline while human annotators did better [75] (**strong**). And **consolidation strips authorisation**: converting history into reusable facts erased the source constraints governing its authorised use in 48 of 49 configurations across seven consolidators and seven backbones, with persisted authority labels driving unauthorised actions from 16.9% to 0.0% at no task cost [76] (**moderate**). On provenance, W3C PROV's durable value is its *conceptual* vocabulary rather than its serialisation [77][78], and richer vocabulary does not produce richer data — four to five orders of magnitude between head and tail classes in one vocabulary of 823 types, the linkability property "deployed by less than 0.02% of all Microdata providers" [79][80] (**strong**). Cited answers are not faithful to their citations, replicated across two independent audits of production systems two years apart: "a mere 51.5% of generated sentences are fully supported by citations and only 74.5% of citations support their associated sentence" [81], ~25–30% of statements unsupported elsewhere [82]. Attribution is explicitly **not a truth claim** [83], and citation metrics have a degenerate optimum — emitting the top-1 passage verbatim scores 99.4/99.4 while fluency collapses [84] (**strong**). Abstention must live **outside** the generator: injecting retrieved context collapses measured abstention from 100%→18.6% and 84.1%→52%, and given *sufficient* context models still hallucinate more often than they abstain [85]. Raw retrieval statistics predict answerability weakly (~0–0.2) against 0.35–0.55 for model uncertainty and 0.71–0.89 for joint predictors [86], while a *learned* retrieval-side sufficiency signal reaches AUROC 0.78–0.81 [87] — the useful axis is **supervision, not side**. Abstention is not free either: selective classification **magnifies group disparities** [88].

## What the evidence does not support

### Claims that trace to no primary source

**"80% (or 90%, or 42%) of organisational knowledge is tacit."** The premise of every pitch in this category, and three citation chains all terminate in non-research artefacts: the 90% variant runs through a 2001 article hedging with "Reportedly" to three trade magazines [89]; the 42% variant to a dead telecoms marketing microsite, its components summing to 88% with the missing 12% never explained [90]; the 80% variant circulates in fixed phrasing with no report title, year, sample or method. The defensible statement is **no primary source is locatable despite targeted searching**. Worse: under Polanyi's actual formulation the quantity is *undefined*, since tacit knowing is an aspect of all knowing rather than a disjoint set of items — "all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge. A wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable" [91] — so there is no partition to take a percentage of, whatever X is.

**"84% of knowledge-management programmes fail."** The primary says the opposite: "about one-sixth of these programs achieve very significant impact within the first two years; half achieve small but important benefits; and the remaining third — the failures — have little business impact." Their own failure figure is **one third**; 84% is 1 minus one-sixth, produced by relabelling the successful half as failures. Nor is it a measurement — it is prefaced "We estimate", with no sampling frame and no impact criterion [92]. The companion **"70% of change initiatives fail"** was traced through its five most prominent sources, each of which states the number without support or cites another that does [93].

**"Knowledge workers spend 2.5 hours a day searching."** The white paper lists this under a heading titled *Assumptions*: "We use a general estimate that the typical knowledge worker spends about 2.5 hours per day… IDC believes the number represents a general average." No survey, sample or instrumentation is reported, the document is marked sponsored by a search vendor, and its own scenario yields $2.5M per 1,000 workers rather than the $5M usually quoted [94].

**"The RRF constant k=60 is principled."** It traces to one sentence in the original short paper — "k = 60 was fixed during a pilot investigation and not altered during subsequent validation" — with a flat pilot sweep, the authors' own note that the choice "was not critical", and no validation on two-way lexical+dense fusion [95]. **"A similarity score above 0.8 means relevant"** is folklore too: no source establishes it for any model, and the values are analytically arbitrary and sometimes not even unique [21].

**"GPT-4-class judges agree with humans as well as humans agree with each other (~80%)."** That is *uncorrected exact-match agreement* [96]; chance-corrected Cohen's κ on the same benchmark is 33–41 percentage points lower across 21 judges and ~541,000 judgments [97]. No chance-corrected number supporting the popular claim exists. Relatedly, **"RAGAS scores can gate a release"**: the entire published validation is 50 questions from 50 Wikipedia pages with 2 annotators, reported as pairwise-preference accuracy, never as calibrated absolute scores [98].

**Two more, briefly.** **"About 40% of entities in an LLM-built knowledge graph are duplicates"** — the 40% is a *chosen hyperparameter*: the study set the entity-reduction ratio, then measured downstream QA [99]. **"RLHF destroys calibration"** traces almost entirely to one figure in one vendor technical report — one model, one multiple-choice benchmark, no methodology, no error bars, no released data [100] — with no peer-reviewed multi-model replication.

**Four absences worth naming rather than filling.** There is no public benchmark or agreed metric for **permission-aware retrieval quality** — no dataset pairs a corpus with per-principal ACLs *and* relevance judgements [101]. There is no study validating **judge–human agreement on any agent-memory benchmark**, so every published memory number rests on a metric unvalidated for its task [96]. The widely-quoted figures for **layered poisoning defences** ("79% → 1%, adaptive 62%") appear in none of the abstracts they are attributed to. And **"post-filtering access control silently collapses recall"** is plausible engineering reasoning routinely presented as a literature finding; no cited paper measures it.

### Claims the literature contradicts

**"Dense search has superseded keyword search."** Every dense retriever averaged *below* BM25 across BEIR, and the 2024 reproduction with much stronger models still concludes "none of the models are viable as a replacement yet" [17] — while its mirror, **"BEIR proves BM25 wins out of domain"**, is disconfirmed by the hole@10 re-judging above [15]. **"More in-domain training will fix dense failure"** is refuted directly: fine-tuning on an in-domain *training* split moved recall@10 from ~0 to 2.8 while fine-tuning on the *test* split solved it, so the models memorise rather than learn the task [20]. And **"an offline metric improvement means users are better off"** [102][28] sits alongside **"neural retrieval has delivered large measurable improvements over classical IR"** — score-standardising every TREC ad hoc submission 1994–2005 against a common reference set found **no upward trend**, and across 109 Robust04 papers 60% reported *best* results below an untuned BM25+RM3 run [103][27].

**"GraphRAG outperforms standard RAG."** The originating result measures comprehensiveness, diversity and empowerment via LLM-as-judge on LLM-generated questions with **no gold answers**, explicitly setting aside faithfulness and answer relevance [104]; on benchmarks with gold answers the picture flattens or reverses [52][49]. What is disconfirmed is *community-summary* GraphRAG, not graph-structured retrieval, and the cheapness claim is mis-stated too: the token comparison is against map-reduce summarisation of the whole corpus, not against vector RAG [104]. **"A knowledge graph gives the model the facts, so hallucination goes down"** is undercut by the graphs' own error rates [50][51].

**"Telling the model to abstain, or labelling credibility in the prompt, is a sufficient safeguard."** Large models "often output incorrect answers instead of abstaining when the context is not [sufficient]" [85]; models notice *that* a conflict exists yet cannot pinpoint it [105]; prompt-level credibility preference reverses under mere repetition [39]; and adding retrieved context collapses measured abstention outright [85]. **"Reordering passages so the trusted one sits at a favoured position fixes it"** — under realistic retrieval, positional bias "is actually marginal in real scenarios" and rearranging by LLM positional preference "do[es] not perform better than random shuffling" [106]; the original lost-in-the-middle result stands for its model generation [107] while a later multi-model benchmark reports most current models robust to it [108] (**contested**).

**"A cleaner index is monotonically better."** The dangerous documents are the retriever's **semantic near-misses**, not the obviously irrelevant ones: adding random documents improved accuracy by up to 35% while high-scoring not-directly-relevant documents hurt [109] — though a direct reproduction shows that effect appearing, weakening or disappearing under small prompt and decoding changes [110]. Nothing reconciles this with the poisoning literature's call for aggressive read-path filtering. Admission control on **provenance** is defensible; admission control on **relevance** must be A/B'd, never assumed beneficial.

**Five security beliefs, all contradicted** (evidence in *Permissions and leakage* above): "post-filtering after ranking is safe" [58][59]; "suppressing counts or refusing small result sets prevents inference" [65]; "hashing identifiers hides which items exist" [66]; "storing embeddings instead of text is privacy-preserving" [68][69]; and "RAG is the privacy-safe alternative to fine-tuning" — 116 of 250 untargeted prompts produced verbatim matches from a retrieved private datastore [111]. Conversely **"early binding is unsafe because indexed ACLs go stale"** is *overstated*: staleness is a per-request consistency parameter with a published solution, and late binding has the same failure if its check snapshot is unbounded [60].

**"Commercial memory layers substantially outperform plain retrieval."** An independent reproducible testbed found the leading commercial system, plain RAG and full-context clustered at 77–81% with two graph systems at 55–56% — inverting the vendor ordering — with plain RAG matching the top cluster at **8.4× lower total cost of ownership** [112], and one vendor reported being unable to reproduce another's published baseline [113]. **"MemGPT is an established result"**: an arXiv preprint with no peer-reviewed venue, evaluated on a benchmark its own team defined and now near-saturated [114]. **"LOCOMO scores are comparable across papers"**: independent evaluations place systems at 21–54% where system papers self-report 68–99%, and one win vanished once retrieval budget was equalised [115]. **"Expire memories by recency"**: recency was the *worst* retention signal in the one head-to-head found — 0.368 gold-evidence retention against 0.770 for a learned multi-factor model [116] — thin evidence, so read it as "the obvious default is unsupported", not "the opposite is proven"; and the most-copied agent-memory retrieval formula (recency + importance + relevance) was validated against human ratings of the *believability* of simulated behaviour, never against factual retrieval accuracy [117].

## What would change a design decision

| Finding | Evidence | kc choice it bears on |
|---|---|---|
| Oracle-evidence beats full history by 30–55% relative [71] | strong | Build a selection layer, with an oracle-evidence upper bound as a first-class harness number — but not for a bounded corpus that fits the window |
| Reranking +11% on 16/18; hybrid +0.01–0.02 [15][17] | strong | Retrieval budget goes to the reranker; ship hybrid for robustness, never promise accuracy from it |
| Dimension bounds representable top-*k* subsets [20] | strong | Keep a lexical channel permanently; route on query–corpus vocabulary overlap |
| Bi-temporal cannot hold live disagreement [40]; Wikidata's rank model can [41] | strong | N claims per (entity, property), each with source and status; three-value rank; best-rank default view; deprecated retained, excluded, never deleted |
| Post-ranking ACL trimming leaks corpus statistics [58] | strong | Visibility predicate inside the retrieval operator; principal identifiers at ingest, group membership resolved at query time [61] |
| Consolidation stripped authorisation in 48/49 configurations [76] | moderate | Every summarisation or memory-write step propagates provenance and authority; index-time enforcement precedes query-time filtering |
| Graph indexing is 40–60× and recurs; winning methods keep source text [52] | moderate | If built at all, graph structure is a navigation layer over original passages — never replace passages with summaries |
| A false merge is unrestricted substitution: 2 statements → 78 false equivalences [54] | strong | Two-tier identity, transitive only for shared strong identifiers; never materialise closure; merges retractable; canonicalization as a view |
| ~20% missed-match at 0.55% false-match; pairwise F1 hides cluster error [55] | moderate (*unverified*) | Report false-merge and false-split separately per cluster-size bucket; "unresolved" is a terminal state, not a queue |
| Coverage failures dominate; users do not know what they missed [11] | strong | Instrument index coverage as a first-class metric; a confident synthesised answer removes the last cue that something was missed |
| Cheap capture is insufficient; a staffed responder works [3][5] | strong | Do not budget the design around friction reduction; ask what the contributor's accounting unit is |
| Citations are unfaithful (51.5% supported); attribution ≠ truth [81][83] | strong | Attribute-first generation so selected segments *are* the citations; never render "cited" as "verified"; pair citation metrics with one copying destroys [84] |
| Context suppresses abstention; the best gate is a supervised combiner [85][87] | moderate–strong | The abstention decision lives outside the generator; report AURC and coverage-at-target-risk per segment [88] |

## What the literature does not settle

**Corpus size has never been isolated as an experimental variable.** Every "small corpus" datapoint confounds size with domain specialisation and with sparse, differently-constructed relevance judgments, so nothing says whether a few-thousand-document *general-language* corpus behaves like BEIR's small specialist ones [15][18], and nothing fixes BM25's k1/b at that scale [118]. Relatedly, **does a controlled vocabulary still add recall on top of a strong dense or hybrid retriever?** The decisive concept-versus-text evidence all predates dense retrieval and no rigorous modern replication was found [45][46].

**What is the base rate of genuine conflict in a real corpus?** No source measures it: one human-annotated set yields 253 instances from the whole of Wikipedia [119], the large benchmark is 7.4M *synthetic* pairs [120], and every prevalence claim in the field is an assertion. Measuring it on kc's own corpus — NLI over top-k on a query sample — is the highest-value action available before the schema is frozen. Two gaps sit beside it: **no evidence exists that conflict-preserving representations beat latest-wins downstream** at the store level, so keeping disagreement is well-motivated but empirically unvalidated; and **no user study was found** testing whether "sources disagree, here are both" produces better human outcomes than one confident answer, or merely produces abandonment.

**Does the corpus-statistics side channel exist in dense retrieval?** All published attacks target lexical statistics; a shared ANN graph plausibly leaks through traversal behaviour and k-th neighbour distance, but no study was found in either direction [58]. Equally open: **how stale a materialised permission index may safely be** — the mechanism is published, but no risk model or empirical distribution of time-from-revocation-to-harm exists [60] — and **how much an embedding leaks at production chunk length**, since nobody has reconstructed a 512-token chunk from an undisclosed encoder and no reconstruction-versus-length curve has been published [70].

**Does staleness metadata add anything once retrieval-sufficiency and model-confidence features are in the gate?** No study isolates it and reports incremental AUROC [86][87]. The retrieval-versus-model comparison is itself confounded: every strong retrieval-side result is supervised on end-task correctness while the model-side baselines it beats are unsupervised, and **no matched-supervision ablation exists**.

**At what error rate do users stop trusting inferred structure?** The design-rationale literature's acceptance bar — "as long as the inferences are right part of the time and it is apparent to the user when the system has made the wrong inference" — has never been calibrated [121]. Nor is there a published **false-merge rate for a production LLM-constructed knowledge graph**: every quantitative entity-resolution error rate available comes from person-record linkage or curated matching benchmarks, not from text-extracted graphs.
